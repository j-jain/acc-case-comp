# RD00. The answer first: who decides the replacement, and should cricket reward the trade?

Prepared 8 November 2026. Start here for the replacement-decision analysis of the Apollo Round 3 case.

**What this folder is.** A focused follow-up to the three re-analyses (`..\McKinsey_Lens\`, `..\Bain_Lens\`, `..\BCG_Lens\`). The user accepted the diagnosis that Apollo's problem is its share of the replacement market and asked three things: what makes an owner rebuy or switch; whether that choice is consumer-led or distributor-led; and, if distributor-led, whether using cricket to give the trade benefits is the strategy, and whether it has been done. Four research notes were written for it on 8 November 2026 (`..\research_notes\U1` to `U4`, with the coded corpus `U2_replacement_sentiment_corpus.csv`). It is our own analysis. It is not the work of any consulting firm, and no firm had any part in it.

## 1. The answer

> **The tractor-tyre replacement is consumer-led in its reason and trade-led in its close. The owner decides whether to leave a brand, on how long the old tyre lasted; the shop decides most of the time which brand he leaves for, through what is in stock, the price per pair, the credit and its own guarantee, and after a failure the nearest shop with the size decides almost alone. About three in five replacements are closed by the trade. So the user's strategy is right for the close: cricket should reward the dealers, mechanics and tractor dealers who close the sale, tied to stock and to registered tyres, on top of the per-tyre money, and tested against cash. Nobody has done this in farm tyres anywhere; Apollo would be first. It does not replace the owner's side: without a life number he believes, the shop is closing on a brand the owner will not rebuy.**

## 2. Three findings

### 2.1 Nobody has measured who chooses, and the evidence that exists points two ways

- No study in India or abroad measures who picks a tractor tyre at replacement: not J.D. Power, not a tyre maker, not a broker, not a thesis. [Verified fact that none was found]
- Owners and shops disagree everywhere they have been asked. US shop surveys say the shop sets the brand for 72 to 78% of buyers (2003 trucks, 2011 cars); US and Japanese owners credit the salesperson 17 to 36% of the time, and about half arrive knowing the brand. [Benchmark - external]
- Indian tractor owners in public speak as the chooser: in 261 coded items, self 61%; a tyre dealer in 8%, though 14 of those 22 items are dealers' own videos and comments and no owner says a dealer chose; mechanic once. Tyre life is the reason in 42% overall and 52% of first-hand reports. Owners do not say "the dealer told me"; dealers in their own videos sell on price per pair, their shop's guarantee and credit. [Outside-in count]
- The trade-side voices are thinner than they first looked. CEAT's marketing head said "the influencer is the garage mechanic who explains to you that your tyre is damaged" (afaqs, 2020), which describes the urgent purchase; the trade press calls tyre decisions "often dealer-led" (Social Samosa, 2026). In farm shops for other inputs, credit and stock-outs hand the choice to the shop: 92.5% switch when the brand is missing and 65% follow the dealer who gives credit (Kurnool, pesticides). [Reported by press; Fact - single source]

### 2.2 The decision has layers, and Apollo loses both the owner's and the shop's

| Layer | Who holds it | Best estimate | Apollo today |
|---|---|---|---|
| The reason to leave or stay | The owner | Strongest evidence of any driver | No life number in circulation; six of eight items naming Apollo as the factory tyre are failure or claim stories, through the tractor dealer |
| The shortlist | The owner, with neighbours and video | Owner-led | Named in 16% of the 261 items, against MRF 29% and Goodyear 28%; no reason given for choosing Apollo at replacement |
| The close, planned | The shop | About half the purchases trade-led (band 40 to 60%) | Thin stocking (about 170 listed dealers against about 3,690 for MRF); no farm scheme |
| The close, after a failure | The mechanic and the nearest shop | About four in five trade-led (band 70 to 85%) | Not on the shelf where it is not stocked |
| **All replacements** | | **About three in five closed by the trade** (band 50 to 70%) | Pull-through from its own fitment at most 30% if Apollo is on 30% of tractors reaching first replacement, below the weakest US car segment |

[Interpretation; analogue-based, not a measurement; Computed for the pull-through ceiling]

### 2.3 Cricket for the trade: right, new in farm tyres, and unmeasured anywhere

- **Has it been done?** In farm tyres, nowhere. In Indian tyres, lightly: Apollo's consumer Champions Offer ran through dealers' counters for car and two-wheeler tyres; BKT lists "dealer activations"; CEAT has a ticket pool and no published dealer use; in fourteen months Apollo has published no dealer-only, sales-linked scheme. Elsewhere: paint makers' dealers and painters met IPL players; India Cements' consumer offers gave buyers of 50 bags (2018) and 25 bags (2023) an assured gift and a chance, by contest or lucky draw, of match tickets; Gulf Oil took mechanics to an IPL match; Bridgestone's US dealers who reach "certain milestones" can use tickets, hospitality and, for a fee, athlete appearances. [Verified fact; Reported by press]
- **Did it work?** Nobody has published a controlled result. The only sales figure is Firestone's uncontrolled 30 to 50% (2006). The controlled evidence is indirect: in repeated ranked sales tournaments, tangible prizes beat cash because losers kept trying; sponsorship leverage moved fans only. Our inference from it: slabs every dealer can reach rather than one ranking, cricket on top of the money, and a test against cash. [Benchmark - external; Reported by press; Interpretation]
- **The calendar decides the design.** India plays no home international from March 2027 to the reported England series in January 2028, which covers both 2027 buying seasons. So the December 2026 to March 2027 home matches reward stocking before the first season; the season rewards are immediate (shirts, money) and the seats come in early 2028. [Verified fact for the calendar; Strategic hypothesis]

## 3. What changes in the plan

| # | Change | Cost to March 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cricket rewards for the trade become the jersey's main farm use: a stocking reward (December to March), season slabs for dealers, a shirt slab for mechanics, hospitality for tractor-dealer principals, a fitter's voucher | About Rs 1.0 crore, replacing the Bain lens's Rs 0.9 crore top-300 hospitality |
| 2 | No stock, no reward | Nil |
| 3 | A fast lane for factory-Apollo claims through the tractor dealer, inside the seven-day clock | Inside the claims system |
| 4 | Life numbers from registered owners as the message that travels | Inside the content line |
| 5 | A counter intercept at 40 counters, the first measurement of who chooses | About Rs 0.1 crore |
| 6 | Village tournaments made optional | Saves about Rs 0.9 crore if dropped |

**Option D becomes about Rs 15.2 to 15.7 crore to March 2028, or about Rs 14.3 to 14.8 crore without the tournaments.** The national offer breaks even at about 1.3 points before the slabs, and at about 1.7 points with their Rs 2.8 crore a year at scale unless they pay for themselves (RD02 section 6.3). The savings assume option D carries the Bain lens's hospitality and tournaments at full cost; the Bain budget carries Rs 2.2 crore for its four cricket parts, which add to Rs 2.73 crore, so check before the figures go on a slide. On the build in `..\..\Apollo_R3_Final_Answer_Summary.md` (about Rs 15.4 crore) that is about Rs 15.6 crore, or about Rs 14.7 crore without the tournaments. That summary's slide 4 also carried one error, "four in ten owners make two-thirds of purchases" (a share of tractor hours in the Punjab survey, not of purchases); the summary was updated with these findings and that correction on 8 November 2026. [Computed] Detail: `RD03` section 4.
