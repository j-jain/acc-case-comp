## 1. "Is sports sponsorship worth it?"

- **Source:** [McKinsey, 1 June 2014](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/is-sports-sponsorship-worth-it), by Jeff Jacobs, Pallav Jain and Kushan Surana. United States context; twelve years old.
- **The problem it names:** industry research (not McKinsey's own survey) finds that about one-third to one-half of US companies have no system to measure sponsorship return comprehensively. In McKinsey's experience, a comprehensive approach can raise returns by as much as 30%.
- **First step it prescribes:** state a sponsorship strategy: the objective of the portfolio, the target group, and which stages of the consumer decision journey (awareness, consideration, purchase, loyalty) the sponsorship is meant to support.
- **Five measures:**

| # | Measure | What the article says |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cost per reach | Count rights fees, activation and advertising as cost. Count reach among the target group, not total reach. One retailer found 15% of its properties cost twice the average per person reached, reallocated, and raised reach 20% at the same cost. |
| 2 | Unaided awareness per reach | Companies often buy rights and spend little on activation. Per dollar of rights, activation ranged from USD 0.50 to 1.60 (McKinsey's experience and IEG research, 2011). One consumer-goods company spending 80% on rights and 20% on activation shifted money to activation and raised unaided awareness of its best properties by 15%. |
| 3 | Sales or margin per dollar | Two routes: (a) link sponsorship to survey measures such as unaided awareness, willingness to consider and propensity to buy, then link those to sales; (b) econometrics over a long period. A handset maker using route (a) found a tenfold difference in return between its best and worst quartile of sponsorships. |
| 4 | Long-term brand attributes | Check by survey which brand attributes each property supports. The article states that brand strength contributes 60 to 80% of overall sales. Some of the handset maker's properties were misaligned with its brand and had negative returns. |
| 5 | Indirect benefits | For example hosting clients or trade partners at sponsored events. Often either ignored or overstated. A bank found client hosting at one golf tournament covered the sponsorship's cost. |

- **Use for Apollo:** measure the jersey on reach among tractor owners, not total reach; set activation against the rights fee; link to consideration first, then sales; treat dealer and mechanic hosting as the "indirect benefit" and count it. [Interpretation]

## 2. "The consumer decision journey"

- **Source:** [McKinsey Quarterly, 1 June 2009](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey), by David Court, Dave Elzinga, Susan Mulder and Ole Jørgen Vetvik. Seventeen years old.
- **Evidence base:** purchase decisions of almost 20,000 consumers in five industries (cars, skin care, insurance, consumer electronics, mobile telecoms) on three continents.
- **Scope stated by the authors:** markets with varied media, internet access and wide product choice, "including big cities in emerging markets such as China and India". Applying it to rural tractor owners is our adaptation, not the authors' claim. [Interpretation]
- **The model:** a loop of four phases, not a funnel: initial consideration; active evaluation; closure (the purchase); post-purchase experience.
- **Findings used here:**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| The starting set is small and decisive | Brands in the initial consideration set can be up to three times more likely to be bought than brands outside it. |
| Brands can still enter later | During active evaluation, car shoppers added 2.2 brands to an initial set of 3.8; computer shoppers added 1 to an initial 1.7. |
| Most influence during evaluation is not the company's | Two-thirds of touch points in active evaluation are consumer-driven: word of mouth, reviews, in-store interactions, memory of past experience. One-third are company-driven. |
| The funnel still has a use | It shows a brand's strength against rivals at each stage and where adoption stalls. |
| Loyalty comes in two kinds | The article distinguishes loyalists who stay and recommend from those who stay passively and can be won away (read in the part of the page beyond the first 9,500 characters; not re-read here). [Single source; partly read] |

- **Use for Apollo:** the jersey works on the initial consideration set; the dealer, the mechanic and other owners are the consumer-driven touch points that decide active evaluation and closure; registration and follow-up build the post-purchase loop. [Interpretation]

## 3. "Indian farmers' optimism is driving interest in new products"

- **Source:** [McKinsey, 15 January 2025](https://www.mckinsey.com/in/our-insights/indian-farmers-optimism-is-driving-interest-in-new-products), by Nitika Nathani, Pradeep Prabhala, Dwijo Goswami and Akshita Agarwal.
- **Evidence base:** survey of 1,031 Indian farmers in early 2024, part of a global survey of 4,400 producers; compared with a similar 2022 survey. The article does not say how many respondents own tractors.
- **Findings:**

| Topic | 2022 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Expect higher profits over the next two years | 37% | 76% |
| Pay digitally at retail (mostly in person, by QR code) | 11% | more than 40% |
| Use crop insurance | 8% | about 37% |
| Use bank financing | 9% | 36% |

- Three-quarters of farmers cite interest rates as an obstacle to formal credit.
- More than 95% have not adopted modern farming technology.
- Indian farmers were the least keen of those surveyed globally on digital interaction: only one in ten prefers it at every stage of a purchase. Barriers named: poor customer service and payment terms that do not fit.
- Farmers invest where they see a clear return. The article recommends stronger on-the-ground demand generation, in-person plus digital service, and loyalty programmes to encourage digital use.
- **Use for Apollo:** supports an in-person, dealer-first model with digital in support; supports stating the tyre offer as a return (cost per hour); supports credit as part of the offer, since interest rates are the named obstacle. The 2024 optimism predates the weak 2026 monsoon. [Interpretation]
