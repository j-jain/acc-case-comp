## 0. What the judges are actually asking for

The brief asks for one major festival, one recurring tradition or ritual, one form of entertainment, and key assumptions, judged on **creative, authentic, well-structured**, with the thinking process visible.

| Judge's word | What will earn it | What will lose it |
|---|---|---|
| Creative | A festival that could only exist on Lumen, generated by Lumen's own facts | The first ideas every team will have: a festival of lights, a moon festival, a masquerade, a stargazing night, a coming-of-age at 50 |
| Authentic | Every element traces to one of the 11 facts; no Earth festival in disguise; numbers that hold (416-day year, 30-hour day, 1,000 cap, 10-hour sleep) | Diwali with the serial numbers filed off; a harvest festival on a planet with no seasons; a stadium for 50,000; a schedule that forgets people sleep |
| Well-structured | The three components interlock, and the slide shows the chain "fact → insight → design" | Three unrelated ideas listed side by side |

One result from anthropology makes the brief's own structure make sense. Harvey Whitehouse's "modes" work shows cultures bond people through two kinds of ritual: **rare, high-arousal events** (which fuse small groups through shared intense memory) and **frequent, low-arousal practices** (which hold large populations together by habit). The brief's "one major festival" is the first kind; "one recurring tradition" is the second; "one form of entertainment" is the play that fills the hours between. A strong answer treats the three as one system.

A note on the judges. ACC is the Ashoka Consulting Club (founded 2017). The only competition of theirs visible online is an inter-university case competition run with the De Beers Group; whether this edition is that one is unverified (check the competition mail). If it is, remember that De Beers is the most famous culture designer in marketing history: the diamond engagement ring and the 1947 line "A Diamond Is Forever" are an invented tradition that made a stone carry meaning. A judge from there will spot a borrowed festival instantly and will respect a design that makes the precious gas carry meaning, not only value.

A rule for the whole document: the Indian and world references below are for the team's reasoning and the Q&A. **None of them goes on the slide.** The case bans Earth festivals; a judge in India will recognise any of them by name or by form.

## 1. The thinking process: from 11 facts to a backbone

### 1.1 The symptom is specific

The case does not say "they are unhappy". It says people **finish work and go home alone**, gatherings are **close to empty**, and life is **"functional but empty"**. Three distinct failures:

1. No destination after work (the "third place" problem; Oldenburg, Putnam).
2. Nothing to **look forward to** (the case says this in so many words).
3. No shared story (no past; nothing to belong to).

Each design below must answer all three; the matrix in section 5 scores them on it.

### 1.2 The backbone every design can share (stated as assumptions)

| Element | Proposal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Year** | 416 Lumen days (520 × 24 ÷ 30) | Arithmetic from fact 1. |
| **Moon** | Assume the moon's cycle is **32 Lumen days** → **13 moons** a year, exactly (13 × 32 = 416, no leftover day) | The case gives one moon but no period, so we may choose one that makes the calendar whole. Kodak ran a 13-month calendar of 28-day months from 1928 to 1989 and needed a "Year Day" outside the weeks; that blank day is exactly what sank the 13-month calendar's League of Nations bid in 1937 (a day outside the week makes every weekly observance drift). Lumen's 13 × 32 has no such flaw. |
| **Week** | 8 days (4 weeks per moon, **52 weeks** a year), with **2 shared rest days** at the end of each week (the "rest pair") | France's 1793 calendar put one rest day in ten and died within twelve years; the Soviet 1929 "continuous week" staggered everyone's rest days and died because, as one worker wrote to Pravda, it is no holiday if you have to have it alone. Rest must be frequent and **synchronised**. Six 30-hour work days are 180 hours (7.5 Earth days), so the two rest days sit together. |
| **Day** | 30 hours: 15 light (hours 0–15), 15 dark (hours 15–30); sleep 10 of the dark hours → **5 waking dark hours every day** ("the dark hours", hours 15–20) if sleep is exactly 10 hours and all of it in the dark; longer sleep means fewer, which is why "at least one 10-hour stretch" goes on the assumptions list | Facts 1 and 9. This is the natural slot for daily culture: everyone is awake, it is dark, work is over. Earth evidence that a waking spell inside a long night is natural: in Wehr's 1992 study, people given 14 hours of darkness slept in two bouts with one to three waking hours between. Humans have been entrained to days beyond 29 hours only under very bright light, so Lumenite biology is assumed native to the 30-hour day. |
| **Seasons** | None (constant 15/15 implies no tilt). Any "season" is an invention and is labelled as one. | Earth's calendars are harvest calendars; Lumen's cannot be. Time is marked by the moon, the sky, and counting. |
| **Life** | Thresholds at **50** (adult), **150** (half-life), **250** (elder); each birth-year cohort ≈ **167 people** (50,000 ÷ 300, assuming a stable population); a 50-year span is a **Ring** (six Rings alive at once) | Fact 11. A cohort fits in the 1,000 room with family. A person meets each threshold once, but sees six cohorts pass through each one. |
| **Crowd** | 50,000 = 50 × 1,000. Open air is uncapped (50,000 at one per square metre need five hectares; crush risk only begins near five per square metre); homes exist; **1,000 streets of ~50 people** is a workable civic unit | Fact 3. The room is the scarce resource; the sky and the street are not. |
| **Money** | Precious gases = noble gases (neon, argon, krypton, xenon, helium): invisible, inert, they **glow in distinct colours when charged in a sealed low-pressure vessel**, they **leak**, and helium, once released, **leaves a planet for good**. Assume household **lighting runs on the same gases that serve as money**, so to light a room is to spend | Fact 4. "Lumen" is the SI unit of light. Wealth that glows and escapes is the most Lumen-native material in the case. Glow colours verified: neon red-orange, argon violet-blue, helium peach, xenon blue, krypton pale lavender. A 30-metre neon sign holds only about 0.4 litres of gas, so a "breath" of gas is a coin-sized unit. |

### 1.3 Fourteen design rules the research hands us

Each rule is a precedent, not an opinion. The scenarios are tested against these.

1. **Simultaneity beats co-location.** Diwali's lamps, Ramadan's iftar at the same minute, Chhath's dawn in every pond, Bali's Nyepi: a whole population does one act at one moment and the city becomes the venue. Benedict Anderson's point about nations: strangers feel like one people through simultaneous private acts. This is the primary answer to the 1,000-person cap.
2. **Synchronised rest is the substrate of shared culture** (Soviet failure, 1929–1940). Pure rotas destroy shared time. Any relay design needs a synchronous element on top.
3. **Never remove rest, and never space it thinly** (French Republican calendar, 1793–1806). The French year had about as many free days as a Gregorian one (54 against 52 or 53) but spaced one in ten; the spacing, not the total, killed it. Lumen's 8-day week keeps its two rest days together.
4. **Scarcity creates anticipation.** Singapore allocates free National Day Parade seats by ballot and the ballot makes a state ritual feel like a prize; Hemis unrolls its giant thangka only at dawn and only every 12th year; Eid's date is confirmed only when the moon is actually seen. The 1,000 room should be a prize, not a problem.
5. **Re-perform the founding act.** Dhamma Chakra Pravartan Din re-enacts the 14 Oct 1956 conversion every year; Vaisakhi re-stages 1699's call for five volunteers. Lumen's Year 1 is its only myth; design it to be repeated.
6. **Ritualise their own work.** Kibbutz Omer festivals (from 1946) built rites out of the members' actual labour and they felt authentic; Findhorn's one-minute circle at the start of every shift lasted 60 years. Lumenites love work; build with it, not against it.
7. **Seed, don't legislate.** Burning Man's Ten Principles were written in 2004 to describe a culture that already existed, and they exported; an imposed code (Bhutan's 1989 dress law) was resented by a minority, and about a hundred thousand of them left the country in the early 1990s. The Council should launch a practice and write the principles in Year 10.
8. **Costly is sticky.** Chhath's 36-hour waterless fast, Thaipusam's piercings, Burning Man's desert: costly rituals bond harder and recruit more giving (Sosis; Xygalatas 2013). A comfortable people ("food, shelter, work taken care of") needs a cost to feel anything: lost sleep, released gas, physical strain.
9. **Capture is the default failure of a capitalist festival.** Mother's Day was captured by retail within its founder's lifetime (she died fighting it); Onam now records liquor sales; Ganesh immersion needed a High Court order on plaster. Write the anti-commercial rule on day one (Burning Man: nothing is sold).
10. **Rivalry spreads a festival and also fractures it.** Durga Puja's 40,000+ neighbourhood committees and Ganesh mandals scaled through competition; Manipur's Sangai festival collapsed when the state fractured. Use rivalry, and reshuffle the teams.
11. **Dense populations prefer calendar-fixed rites** (Reeves & Bylund 1989, cross-cultural). Lumen is one dense settlement: anchor the festival on the calendar and let sky events be a bonus, not the only trigger.
12. **Reconcile on a schedule.** Jain Kshamavani asks everyone's forgiveness once a year, quarrel or not; Bastar's Muria Darbar has heard village grievances before the ruler every year since 1876. People who will know each other for 300 years need both.
13. **Name a carrier institution.** Santiniketan's festivals outlived Tagore because Visva-Bharati runs them; Maghotsav survived its founder's exit because a building's anniversary needs no founder. Kwanzaa faded with its movement and founder. Lumen's culture must not belong to the Council personally, and must not depend on the Head.
14. **Make a past by counting forward.** Hobsbawm's invented traditions claim a fictitious antiquity; Lumen cannot. But a 300-year life means a single person will do the same rite 300 times: on Lumen a tradition becomes ancient within one lifetime. Number the years from 1 and let the count be the history.

Designed-culture lessons that matter most (from the second report): Auroville was planned for exactly **50,000** residents and has about 3,300 after 58 years (a plan without pull); Burning Man's **~70,000** is the closest Earth analogue to a from-scratch culture of Lumen's size, and it runs on gifting, a fire climax, and principles written after the fact; Tagore's Santiniketan cycle (Poush Mela 1894, tree planting from 14 Jul 1928) survived a century because an institution carries it; Hornbill (2000) is criticised as staged because it was built for spectators, not participants (Lumen has no spectators: everyone is a participant, which is an advantage).

## 3. The five designs

Each design is a complete answer: one festival, one ritual, one entertainment, assumptions, then the stress tests. Each is generated by a **different** fact of the case, so they are vividly different in texture: sky, memory, room, dark, work.

Template for the stress tests: **Runs** (Year 1, 10, 50, 300; a worker's 30-hour day; the bad year; the Council's objection), **Problems**, **Shortfalls**, **Advantages**, **Indian references** (mechanism borrowed → form changed) with **copy-risk** as an Indian judge would see it, a **scorecard** (1–5 on creative, authentic, well-structured; the blind judge's pre-fix scores are in section 8), and a one-line slide headline.

### Design 1: THE THIRTEENTH MOON (the sky is the calendar)

**Generating fact:** 8 (astronomical events are common), with 1, 2 and 6 (15-hour nights, one moon, Sky as an element).
**Generating insight:** Lumen has no seasons and no past, but it has a sky that changes every night and a population that is awake for five dark hours a day. Make the sky the calendar, and make reading it the culture. Twelve of the thirteen moons are grouped into four invented quarters named for the ground elements (Water, Air, Fire, Earth, three moons each: they are labels for counting, not climates); the thirteenth moon belongs to Sky and sits outside the four quarters. The Parsi calendar's five Gatha days and the Bahá'í Ayyám-i-Há are "days outside the months"; Lumen gets a whole moon outside the quarters. The calendar itself is fixed at 416 days; the sky confirms it, it does not float it.

**Festival: FIRST RISING (the last night of the Sky Moon).**
- All 32 nights of the Sky Moon, the city watches: no work after dusk, the dark hours are spent outdoors, and every night the Watch reads out, in the 1,000 room and on the open ground, what the sky did that night. Ramadan's mechanism (a whole month with one synchronised daily rhythm) with none of its form.
- The year has a **Year Star**: a bright star chosen in Year 1 whose reappearance at the edge of dawn (its "first rising") recurs every 416 days. On a planet with no tilt it is the only annual sky event that is exactly periodic.
- On the final night nobody sleeps (a 30-hour waking day: the one costly night a year). Everyone is outdoors. The first person to see the Year Star cries out, and that cry **declares the number of the year** ("Thirty-one!"). The number is the planet's whole history; whoever first sees the star gets to say it. If cloud hides the star, the waking night happens anyway on its fixed date and the Count is declared at the first clear dawn after; the year does not drift, but a "Late Rising" year is remembered as one, and the waiting is part of the festival (Eid's date is confirmed only when the moon is seen; Lumen's calendar is not).
- Then the city performs its one synchronised sleep: the **Still**, ten hours when all 50,000 sleep at once, through the first light of the new year, and the settlement is silent (Nyepi's mechanism, a day of nothing, in Lumen's own biology).

**Ritual: THE WATCH (every night, 300 people in three shifts).**
- Every night, three shifts of 100 keep the whole 15-hour dark (hours 15–20, 20–25, 25–30), so the dawn shift is awake for the Year Star and for anything that happens while the city sleeps. By rota, each citizen serves once every 167 nights (about 750 watches in a life, with nearly everyone on the planet more than once). They record the sky in the **Sky Book** and hold the **Cry**: if something rare happens (an eclipse of the moon, a meteor storm, a bright aurora), the Watch cries the city awake and everyone comes outdoors. The Cry may be raised at most once a moon, so it stays rare. Iranshah's fire is tended five times a day by a few; Lumen's sky is tended every night by three hundred, and the three hundred are everyone in turn.
- Why it works on the symptom: 100 strangers a shift, for five hours, with a job.

**Entertainment: THE NAMING (once a moon, in the room).**
- Lumen's sky has no names. On the last rest-day of each moon: 20 teams of up to 10 present one object or pattern they found in the sky that moon, with a name and a five-minute story; the other 800 seats go by ballot and each seat holds one token; the most tokens wins. The winner's name goes into the Sky Book; the Watch confirms the object exists. Rule: nothing may be named after a living person (Kwanzaa and Children's Day show what person-linked rites cost).
- Thirteen names a year; 3,900 in 300 years: a mythology produced bottom-up by a game. Kite-fighting makes the sky an arena; dastangoi and Pandavani make one voice carry a story; Lumen's version is a naming duel.

**Key assumptions:** 32-day moon; a Year Star exists and is visible at dawn; clear skies are common enough that a Late Rising is rare; "common sightings" means several notable events a year, not nightly; the Watch is unpaid and counts as rest, not work; the four quarters are invented labels, since Lumen has no climatic seasons.

**Runs**
| Run | What happens |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | The Council names the Year Star and the Count begins at 1. The first Sky Moon is awkward: people treat the Watch as a shift. The first Cry (an eclipse in moon 7) is the founding memory: 50,000 people outdoors at once for the first time. |
| Year 10 | 130 named sky objects; children (anyone under 50) learn the sky by its stories. The Watch has become the place you meet people outside work. Risk visible: some Naming teams are really firms advertising. Rule 9 applied: no team may be a company. |
| Year 50 | The first cohort born after Year 1 turns adult and keeps its first Watch. "The year the moon went red" (Year 23) is how people date their lives. The Count is the history. |
| Year 300 | The founders are dead. 3,900 names; the Sky Book is the planet's scripture-without-religion. Stars drift over centuries and the Watch quietly records the shift: the culture now owns its own time. |
| A worker's day | Hours 0–15 light: work. Dusk: home. Hours 15–20: the dark hours, on the street or at the Naming; once every 167 nights, a five-hour Watch shift (the night shifts sleep late or early that day: "at least one 10-hour stretch" allows it). Hours 20–30: sleep. |
| The bad year | A year of cloud: the waking night happens on its date, the Count waits nine dawns for a clear sky, and "the Late Rising of Year 12" is told for a century. A year of no events: no Cry at all; the Naming carries the year. |
| Council objection | "Night watches are labour; people will skip them." Answer: the Watch is a rest activity with a social reward, and a ballot seat at the Naming goes first to those who kept their Watch. "Watchers can see the city." Answer: the Watching Ground faces the sky; the Sky Book records only the sky. |

**Problems and failure modes:** cloud; desensitisation if events really are nightly (the once-a-moon Cry cap is the fix); the Naming decays into in-jokes or advertising; a 30-hour waking night is hard on the old (elders may sleep; the cry wakes them). **Shortfalls:** cerebral, cool, not very physical; the economy (gas) and the Head are unused; nothing in it tastes or sweats; "stargazing" is one of the five ideas every team will have, and this design survives that only because of the shouted Count and the Naming. **Advantages:** the only design where the venue is uncapped by construction (the sky); zero cost; produces history and mythology from nothing; anticipation is built into the trigger; it is the design most obviously impossible on Earth.

**Indian references:** Eid and Karva Chauth moon-sighting (suspense), Kumbh's planetary clock and grahan's collective pause (sky triggers), Ramadan's synchronised month (the Sky Moon), Parsi Gatha days (time outside the quarters), Uposatha's moon-day rhythm, Hemis's dawn reveal, kite-fighting's sky arena, dastangoi's one-voice storytelling. **Copy-risk: low to medium.** A judge may say "Eid's moon committee" or "a Ramadan month"; the defence is that no Earth festival declares its year by a shout at a star, and the calendar does not float.

**Scorecard:** creative 4 · authentic 5 · structured 4 (13). **Headline:** "A people with no past count their years by the sky, and name it as they go."

### Design 2: THE CROSSING (a culture of generations)

**Generating fact:** 11 (300-year lives, adult at 50, work till 250) with 10 (no past).
**Generating insight:** Lumen's problem is not just no festivals; it is no *time*. Nothing marks the difference between being 40 and 140. Build the culture on the one thing guaranteed to happen to everyone: the three crossings of a long life (50, 150, 250) and the one that ends it. Then give the planet a memory by making every household write one line a day. "Coming of age at 50" on its own is the idea every team will have; what makes this a design is the chain of testimony, the daily ledger, and the story that never ends.

**Festival: CROSSING DAY (the first day of the year).**
- The year's cohort turning 50 (~167 people) becomes adult; the cohort turning 150 (~167) makes **the Turn**: with a 200-year working life, each person may declare a second trade at 150, and the Turn is where they say it; the cohort turning 250 (~167) becomes elder; the year's dead (~167) are named by their households. Four groups of about 167: a festival the 1,000 room was built for (the principals fill about 700 seats; the rest go by ballot).
- Morning (light hours 0–7): **the Walk**. The new adults walk the whole settlement in one procession; the road is swept ahead of them; every household stands at its door. The festival comes to the people (Rath Yatra's mechanism, Nagar Kirtan's swept road), so all 50,000 take part without a venue.
- Midday (hours 7–12), in the room: **the Tellings**. Each new elder hands one new adult a story: their 200 working years shaped into one telling, which the adult must carry and will retell when they cross at 250. A chain of testimony that never breaks (Akhand Path hands over mid-line). The Turn-makers declare their second trades. The dead are named.
- Evening (the dark hours), everywhere: **the Unburdening**. Every person asks forgiveness of three people, quarrel or no quarrel (the Jain mechanism, which a 300-year life needs more than Earth does; see copy-risk). And once per Ring, in Years 50, 100, 150 and so on, **the Clearing**: no personal debt between citizens may outlive fifty years; anything older is void. Capitalist economies have statutes of limitation; Lumen's is a Ring long, announced in Year 1 so that lending prices it in.

**Ritual: THE DAY LINE (every night, every household).**
- Before the household sleeps, one person writes **one line** about the day in the house's Ledger. Thirty seconds. On the last night of each moon, the street's ~50 people read their moon's lines aloud at a shared table. Over 300 years every house holds a 124,800-line book; the planet's history is 50,000 house-ledgers. Diwali's new account books, kolam's daily minute at the threshold, and the Panchang's daily entry, with none of their forms.
- The best lines of the year are read on Crossing Day. "No past" is solved mechanically, from below, every night.

**Entertainment: THE LONG STORY (every night, in the room).**
- One story, told one chapter a night, that never ends. Each night a Teller (chosen by the previous night's audience from three volunteers) continues from the last line and must end on a question. The 999 other seats go **one per street**: each of the 1,000 streets sends one listener a night (the Teller covers their own street), rotating within the street, and that listener's duty is to retell the chapter at the street table before the next night. Coverage is complete by construction: 1,000 streets × 50 people = the whole city hears every chapter within a day, with no broadcast. No living person may be a character.
- After 300 years: 124,800 chapters. The epic Lumen never inherited, written by everyone. IPL gives a city nightly anticipation for two months; this gives it forever. Dastangoi's cliffhangers, Pandavani's one voice, the Akhand Path's hand-over.

**Key assumptions:** stable population (~167 per birth-year); households and streets of about 50 are the civic units; literacy; people die (the case's "no history" is about tradition, not biology); the Ledger is private, the readings are voluntary; the Clearing applies to personal debts between citizens, not to firms.

**Runs**
| Run | What happens |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | The first Crossing is the founding: the Council itself walks as the first "cohort". The first Tellings are raw (the elders have lived 250 years but have never shaped a story for telling); the first Long Story chapter is terrible and everyone retells it anyway, which is the joke that binds. |
| Year 10 | 10 cohorts of adults carry Tellings; 4,160 chapters; street readings have become the moon's social event. Risk: the Long Story is captured by a clique of good Tellers. Fix: the vote and the one-seat-per-street rule. |
| Year 50 | The first Clearing. Creditors protest; the Council holds because it was announced in Year 1 (Thanksgiving's lesson: never move a settled date). The first children of Year 1 cross at 50 and receive Tellings from people who remember the founding. |
| Year 300 | The founders cross out. Their Tellings are carried by people who will cross at 250 in Year 500. The Ledgers are the archive; the Long Story is at chapter 124,800. The culture is now older than anyone alive, for the first time. |
| A worker's day | Work in the light; dark hours 15–20: the room (your street's turn) or the retelling at the street table; the Day Line at hour 19; sleep. |
| The bad year | A year with many deaths (an accident): the naming of the dead swamps the Tellings; the Unburdening does real work. A year the Long Story turns ugly (a Teller uses it for politics): the no-living-person rule and the next night's vote correct it. |
| Council objection | "Public naming of the dead; the Clearing is confiscation; cohort rites are ageist." Answer: naming is by the household, opt-in; the Clearing voids only fifty-year-old personal debts and was known from Year 1; the three crossings are for everyone, in turn. |

**Problems and failure modes:** slow payoff (the design is strongest at Year 50, weak at Year 1; Auroville's lesson: a plan without early pull stalls); the Clearing distorts lending near Year 49; Tellings become formulaic; the Long Story needs 416 competent Tellers a year. **Shortfalls:** uses the sky, the gas and the Head barely at all; the entertainment is verbal, so judges who want spectacle will mark it down; the Walk needs route logistics (50,000 people pass a point on a 10-metre road in one to two hours, so a 167-person walk is trivial). **Advantages:** the deepest answer to "no past" and "nothing to belong to"; the three crossings give every person three guaranteed days in a 300-year life when the city turns toward them; the Unburdening and the Clearing are the only explicit answers in any design to what 300-year relationships do to a town of 50,000.

**Indian references:** Kshamavani, the samskara and ashrama life-stage system, Diwali's account books, Rath Yatra and Nagar Kirtan (the festival travels), Akhand Path (unbroken hand-over), Muktad (ten days for the ancestors), dastangoi and Pandavani, Dhamma Chakra Pravartan Din (the founding act re-performed). Japan's Seijin no Hi (the whole cohort turning 20 in one hall, a state-designed rite since 1948) is the closest form. **Copy-risk: medium.** "Ask everyone's forgiveness once a year" is recognisably Jain, and "coming of age" is a cliché; the Turn at 150, the Tellings chain and the Long Story are the parts no Earth festival has. Never use the word "jubilee" anywhere: it is a Biblical institution by name.

**Scorecard:** creative 3 · authentic 5 · structured 4 (12). **Headline:** "Three crossings in a 300-year life, one line a night, one story that never ends."

### Design 3: THE FIFTY NIGHTS (the room is the design)

**Generating fact:** 3 (50,000 people, a room for 1,000) with 4 (gas) and 1 (15-hour nights).
**Generating insight:** every team will see the 1,000 cap as a problem. Treat it as the festival's shape: 50,000 is exactly fifty thousands, so the festival is **fifty nights**, one night for each thousand, and the fiftieth night belongs to everyone. The Soviet failure (staggered rest kills shared time) is answered by a synchronous act every night, and by a single climax. The climax uses the one property of Lumen's money that no Earth currency has: the lightest of the precious gases, once let go, leaves the planet for good.

**Festival: THE FIFTY NIGHTS (the last fifty nights of the year).**
- The settlement is divided into **fifty Fifties** of up to 1,000 people each (contiguous wards; the night's stewards are members of the Fifty, so the room is never over its cap; reshuffled every 50 years at the Ring boundary so they never harden into factions, rule 10).
- Each night for fifty nights, one Fifty holds the room for the dark hours: its own music, its own food, its own stories, judged by no one. Entering the room is the thing: every citizen is inside it once a year, guaranteed. Before leaving, each of the thousand gives **one breath** of gas from their own holdings (a fixed small measure, the same for everyone, so the poor and the rich give alike) into **the Breath**: a great envelope on the open ground that **visibly swells, night after night, as the city's money fills it** (Onam's pookalam grows one ring a day toward the climax; the Breath grows one Fifty a night). The night's Fifty guards it until the next Fifty takes over.
- The synchronous act (rule 2): at the hour the breath is given, the whole city **dims**. 49,000 people stand in the dark and watch the room's glow and the swelling shape on the ground; the thousand inside know the city is watching. Nobody is "off-duty" on another Fifty's night.
- Night 50, the last night of the year: the champion Fifty (below) holds the room, and the whole city holds the ground. Every light on Lumen goes out, and the Breath, full of fifty thousand breaths of helium, is **let go**. It rises into the dark and is gone. Helium does not come back: it leaves the planet. Once a year, the city watches its money leave the world, together, in silence. (If the team wants light: a single breath of charged neon inside the envelope makes it a rising ember; the dark version has the lower copy-risk.)
- Why the dark matters: on Earth, the festivals of light put a lamp on every house. On Lumen, where light is money, the festival darkens every house and spends one shared breath on the sky. It is the inverse, and the inversion is the authenticity.

**Ritual: THE TABLE OF TEN (every rest-eve, in homes).**
- On the eve of each week's rest pair, every person eats one meal with nine people **not of their household, drawn by lot from the whole city**. 5,000 tables of ten in 5,000 homes; you host every tenth week and feed nine (a small cost, rule 8); the old and the ill host from their beds, and their table comes to them. Langar's row of strangers, without the kitchen or the creed; the Golden Temple proves one city's worth of people can be fed by turn every day.
- The arithmetic is the argument: 52 tables a year, ~15,600 in a life; you share a table with every person on the planet roughly three times before you die. "Finish work and go home alone" becomes impossible once a week by design.

**Entertainment: GLOWBALL (the dark hours, any open ground).**
- A night sport. The ball is a sealed sphere of charged gas and is the only light on the field; players, goals and lines are unlit. You walk the ground in daylight to learn it, then play it in the dark from memory. Two teams of ten; spectators see only the ball's path. Each Fifty fields a team; the season runs the first eleven moons; the league table fixes the order of the Fifty Nights, and the champion Fifty takes night 50. IPL's city franchises and two-month anticipation; kabaddi's one rule that is also a clock; the ball's charge is paid by the Fifty's pooled gas (barowari).

**Key assumptions:** the settlement can be partitioned into fifty contiguous wards; "one breath" is a fixed measure of about four litres (one full exhalation) drawn from a person's gas holdings, a coin-sized unit (a 30-metre neon sign holds only about 0.4 litres); fifty thousand breaths are about 200 cubic metres, an envelope seven metres across, which rises easily; the last fifty nights are accepted as a season of shorter working days; helium is one of the precious gases; the Breath is sealed and guarded between nights; noble gases glow only while charged in a sealed low-pressure vessel, which is what the Glowball is.

**Runs**
| Run | What happens |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | The Fifties are drawn by lot. Nobody knows Glowball's rules; the first season is chaos and the chaos is the bonding (Burning Man's rules came after the culture). The first Breath is small and wobbles. The first Table of Ten is awkward; by the tenth, people have a stranger they look forward to. |
| Year 10 | Fifties have names, colours (the gas of their Glowball), and grudges. Night order is contested. A Fifty tries to buy a better team with gas: rule 9, a cap on team gas. |
| Year 50 | First reshuffle: the Fifties are redrawn. Outcry, then relief: rivalries had hardened. The reshuffle itself becomes a rite ("Reshuffle Night"). |
| Year 300 | Six reshuffles. Three hundred Breaths have left the planet; people describe childhood by "the year the Breath caught the wind". The Table of Ten has made the settlement a town where nobody is a stranger. |
| A worker's day | Light: work. Dark hours: Glowball (player or watcher) three nights a week; rest-eve: the Table; during the Fifty Nights: your Fifty's night once, the city dim every other night at the hour of the breath. |
| The bad year | A crush at the room's door on a popular Fifty's night (Kumbh's lesson): entry is by the Fifty's roll, no guests, so the cap is a safety feature. A gas price spike makes a breath dear: the measure is fixed in volume, not value, so it tracks the economy. A storm on night 50: the Breath waits, guarded, for the first calm night, and the year ends when it rises. |
| Council objection | "You are throwing money into the sky." Answer: one breath per person per year, equal for all; the only night the economy is visible, and it is visible as a shape leaving, not as wealth staying. "Fifty nights is too long." Answer: Bastar's Dussehra runs 75 days with tasks distributed by village; each person's active duty is one night plus one Table a week. |

**Problems and failure modes:** ward rivalry turning political (Ganesh mandals); a judge who reads any glow as Diwali (hence the dark release); the Breath needs guarding for fifty nights; fatigue in the fiftieth night for the thousand who waited longest; a league that rewards the Fifty with the most young players. **Shortfalls:** uses the sky only as the place the Breath goes and the Head not at all; the Table of Ten is logistically heavy (a lottery of 50,000 every week); the sport needs equipment; a seven-metre sphere is a modest spectacle for a town, and that modesty has to be sold as intimacy. **Advantages:** the clearest "constraint as feature" story in the set; every component interlocks (league → order → nights → Breath; Table → mixing → no factions); every person is guaranteed one night in the room and 52 tables a year; it directly answers "home alone", "nothing to look forward to" and "empty gatherings" with mechanisms rather than hopes; the Breath's growth over fifty nights is a visible countdown the whole city can watch.

**Indian references:** Durga Puja (barowari pooling, para ownership, five nights of visiting), Onam's pookalam (a visible countdown), Akhand Path and the Olympic relay (unbroken sequence), Bastar Dussehra (75 days, duties by village), langar's rows (the Table), IPL's franchise league, Singapore's ballot. **Copy-risk: medium.** The ward-by-ward nights will remind a Bengali judge of para pujas; the eve-of-rest meal of langar; the release of a sphere of anything from an Indian city may evoke Diwali's sky lanterns for a moment. Defences: no shrine, no lamps, no kitchen; the city goes dark rather than lit; what rises is the money, and it does not come back.

**Scorecard:** creative 4 · authentic 5 · structured 5 (14). **Headline:** "The room holds a thousand. So the festival takes fifty nights, and ends when the city lets its money go into the sky."

### Design 4: THE EMPTY CHAIR (nobody knows who is Head, so everyone might be)

**Generating fact:** 5 (a secret Head of Government) with 1 and 9 (15-hour dark, 10-hour sleep).
**Generating insight:** the strangest line in the case is the secret Head. A society whose most powerful person is unknown has a ready-made ethic: treat every stranger as if they might be the Head. Make that the culture. On Earth the king of Puri sweeps the chariot floor to show that power bows; on Lumen, where the king is hidden among the people, **everyone sweeps**. And Lumen needs no masks for its night of equals: it has fifteen hours of dark, and on Lumen light is money, so darkness is the one condition in which everyone is exactly alike.

**Festival: CHAIR NIGHT (one 15-hour night, mid-year).**
- Dusk: **the Sweeping**. As the light goes, every citizen sweeps the street outside their door, at once. The humblest act, done by all, because any one of them might be the Head (Chhera Pahanra inverted).
- The whole night: **the Dark**. Every light on Lumen stays off until dawn. No names, no ranks, no trade, no work. The city moves by voice and touch; nobody can be seen, so nobody can be ranked. Holi's licensed inversion (Marriott found it the one village festival that overturns rank), Theyyam's reversal (the lowest becomes the one worshipped), without colour, costume or mask.
- In the room, in the dark: **the Chair**. The Council (up to fifty) sits with 950 citizens by ballot before one **empty chair**. If the Head has chosen to write, the letter is read by a citizen drawn by lot that minute, by the light of a single breath of charged gas held below the page: the only light on the planet that night, and it shows the page, not the face, so the reader might be the Head and no one can know. If the Head has not written, the chair keeps one minute of silence, and the silence is the ritual. The design does not depend on the Head's cooperation (rule 13). Then the Muria Darbar mechanism: any voice in the dark may state a grievance to the empty chair, and the Council must answer it within one moon.
- Before dawn, in the streets: **the Telling of Hands**. Unseen, people say aloud the unexplained kindness they received this year, and nobody claims credit. The habit it breeds, doing one anonymous good turn for a stranger each moon, is a norm the night encourages, not a second ritual.
- Dawn ends it: the first light is the first time anyone is seen. The city then sleeps the first ten hours of light, the one morning a year Lumen sleeps in (fact 9 is kept: one 10-hour stretch, shifted).

**Ritual: THE DARK HOUR (every night, the whole city).**
- At the first hour of dark (hour 15), every light in the settlement goes out for one hour. Light is money on Lumen (to light your home is to spend gas), so the Dark Hour is the egalitarian hour: rich and poor, Head and citizen, invisible alike. People step outside, because the dark street is where the neighbours are. The sky, otherwise drowned by a glowing capitalist city, appears. Nyepi's total stop and grahan's sutak pause, shrunk to one hour a night and made daily. The Dark Hour is the "third place": a plaza made of darkness, free, every night, for everyone. Chair Night is the Dark Hour grown to a whole night, so the festival and the ritual are one act at two scales.

**Entertainment: THE HUNT (once a moon, in the room, in the dark).**
- A mass deduction game played by voice. 1,000 players by ballot; 50 are secretly made Heads by lot (one in twenty), marked only by a token in the pocket. The room is dark. A Head wins by **governing unseen**: getting ten strangers to do one thing on their word alone before the night ends (move to a corner, sing a line, stand) without anyone naming them as the cause. Each hour, every player may whisper one name to the stewards; at the hour's end the most-named person is lit for a moment by a single breath of gas and asked, Head or not. A found Head takes the empty chair; a wrong name costs nothing. Heads unfound at dawn are "the Unseen", the year's most honoured players. The game teaches the city's one lesson: you cannot see power; you can only notice who people follow. Earth has parlour games of hidden roles; Lumen's version is a civic mirror with its own stakes (the chair), its own medium (the dark) and its own scale (a thousand).

**Key assumptions:** household light runs on the same gases that serve as money, so lighting is spending and darkness is free and equal; a one-hour nightly blackout and one full dark night a year are safe (emergency lighting exempt, stewards with a breath of gas); the Head's letter is optional and the design works without it; the Council is willing to answer grievances in public within a moon; no masks or uniforms are ever mandated (Bhutan's dress law shows what mandated uniformity does).

**Runs**
| Run | What happens |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | The first Dark Hour: people stay indoors and wait it out. By the third moon, streets fill because the dark is the only place anything happens. The first Chair Night: the Head's letter is two lines; it is quoted for a century. Or the Head writes nothing, and the minute of silence is quoted instead. |
| Year 10 | The Hunt is the most-wanted ballot on the planet. Risk: someone claims to have found the real Head. Rule: on Chair Night the Council neither confirms nor denies anything; the mystery is constitutional. |
| Year 50 | A generation has grown up thinking the Dark Hour is nature. Rank at work has softened because for one hour a night nobody has any. The Sweeping is the planet's most-told moment: 50,000 people bending at once as the light fails. |
| Year 300 | The Head has changed several times; nobody knows when. The empty chair has become the symbol of the state (Durkheim: the group stores its effervescence in a symbol). |
| A worker's day | Light: work. Hour 15: lights out, into the street. Hours 16–20: home, or the Hunt once a moon. Hours 20–30: sleep. |
| The bad year | A crime in the Dark Hour (the Council's fear): the Hour is the one hour a night when everyone is outside and together, the opposite of an empty street. A year the Head's letter is leaked early: the reading goes ahead unchanged; the ritual is the reading, not the news. |
| Council objection | "This mocks the office." Answer: the empty chair honours it; Puri's king sweeping the chariot is the most powerful person doing the humblest act, and the office is stronger for it. "A whole night without light is dangerous." Answer: the stewards carry a breath each; the city has rehearsed one dark hour every night of the year. |

**Problems and failure modes:** a culture of anonymity can deepen atomisation if the Dark Hour is spent indoors (the design depends on the street filling, which took Burning Man a decade and Auroville never); the Hunt can tip into real paranoia about the real Head; "light is money" is an assumption the slide must state, because the whole ritual rests on it. **Shortfalls:** no use of the moon, the 300-year life, or the elements; the festival is one night, so anticipation rests on the Hunt and the ballot; a Hunt in the dark is hard to referee. **Advantages:** the most memorable single sentence in the set; the cheapest ritual of all (turning lights off costs nothing and saves gas); the Dark Hour is the only daily, universal, zero-logistics "third place" in any design; the festival and the ritual are the same act at two scales; it directly attacks rank, which on a planet where everyone fills life with work is what empties it; nothing in it needs the Head to agree.

**Indian references:** Chhera Pahanra (the king sweeps) inverted; Holi (licensed inversion, Marriott 1966); Theyyam (the low made high for a night); Muria Darbar (grievances to the ruler, yearly since 1876); Onam's Mahabali visiting every home (the Head might be beside you); Sikh seva and the ideal of unseen charity (the Telling of Hands); Nyepi and grahan's pause (the Dark Hour). **Copy-risk: medium.** A full night without light will be called Nyepi or Earth Hour by a judge who knows them; the Hunt will be called Mafia. Defences: Nyepi is a day of silence at home, this is a night of talk in the street; Earth Hour is an hour a year, this is every night; and the sweeping-at-dusk is the image to lead with. The former version of this design used identical masks; they were removed because a 30-hour masquerade reads as Carnival.

**Scorecard:** creative 4 · authentic 4 · structured 4 (12). **Headline:** "The Head is secret. So one night a year, everyone is: no mask needed, Lumen has fifteen hours of dark."

### Design 5: THE RAISING (a people who love work lay their own past on the ground)

**Generating fact:** 7 (every adult works and fills life with work because there is nothing shared) with 3, 6 and 10.
**Generating insight:** don't fight work; share it. The kibbutzim built festivals out of their own labour and they felt real; Findhorn's one-minute circle before every shift turned work into something done together for sixty years. Lumen has 50,000 willing workers and no monument, no history, no landmark. Give them one day a year to build one thing together, and keep it: not a tower (300 rings of a tower would stand 300 metres and more, and could not be hauled by hand) but **rings on the ground**, one ring a year around the Hall, so that in 300 years the settlement stands inside 300 rings that are literally its history, laid by hand, and the ring you set in your fiftieth year is at a distance you can point to.

**Festival: THE RAISING (one day, the first of the year).**
- The 1,000 room is the Hall at the centre. The first ring was laid a hundred paces out; each year's ring is laid one pace beyond the last. Ring 1 was about 500 metres round (fifty sections of 10 metres); ring 300, four hundred paces out, will be about 2 kilometres round (fifty sections of 40 metres): the work grows as the history grows.
- Every ward sets its own section of the year's ring: that is the universal act. Beyond it, each ward also holds one permanent service (Bastar Dussehra's hereditary task map: named villages build the chariot, supply the rope and pull it; in 2018 four thousand people from 55 villages "stole" the chariot until the king negotiated, which is how much a task can mean): the cutting wards dress the stone in the weeks before (that is their work for the year's last moon), the carrying wards haul, the feeding wards cook, the keeping wards count and record, the inspecting wards (the elders' service) sign the ring. Children (under 50) carry; adults (50–250) set; elders (250+) inspect. No section is set in place before the day.
- Hours 0–12 of the light: the sections are hauled on sledges by each ward's hundred on a rope, to a chant that sets the stroke (Rath Yatra's crowd on the rope; Vallam Kali's hundred rowers), and set in their arc. Hour 12: the ring closes and the year's number is cut into it. Dusk: a channel inside the new ring is charged with the year's gas colour and glows once, and never again (the old rings are dark; the only lit ring is this year's). In Year 1 nobody knew the choreography and the ring closed at dawn; that dawn is the founding memory, and every year since has closed at dusk.
- The dark hours: the city eats in the open, all 50,000 at once (five hectares at one person per square metre), each ward seated on the section it set. If the ring is not closed by dark, it waits for the next light; nobody is denied sleep (fact 9).
- The rings are never broken up (Burning Man burns its Temple; Lumen has nothing to grieve yet and everything to remember).

**Ritual: THE CIRCLE (twice a day, every workplace).**
- At the start and end of every shift, the team stands in a ring and goes round once: each person says in one line what they will do, or did, and the ring answers "seen". One minute. Not silent (a silent hand-held minute reads as prayer on a planet with no religion); spoken, so it is a roll, not a rite. Findhorn's attunement supplied the mechanism, which ran in every department for sixty years. Cost: two minutes a shift. Effect: the thing Lumenites already do all day (work) becomes, twice a day, a thing done *together*, and the Circle on the Raising day, fifty wards each standing round the section it set, is the same gesture scaled up. The ritual and the festival are the same act.

**Entertainment: THE PULL (the rest days, all year).**
- Hundred-person rope teams, one per ward, race to haul a weighted sledge over a marked course to a chant that sets the stroke. Synchrony research (Wiltermuth & Heath 2009) shows moving in time makes strangers cooperate afterwards; the Pull is that effect, practised weekly. The league decides which ward hauls the keystone section at the Raising. Spectators line the course; the chants become the planet's first songs.

**Key assumptions:** a durable building material exists on Lumen (stone or the like; the case says the elements are Earth's); sections are small and pre-dressed so the day is ceremonial rather than dangerous; one pace of radius a year keeps the rings inside the settlement for centuries; wards and services are stable for 50 years then reshuffled; the Council accepts a permanent public monument it does not own; the Circle counts as work time.

**Runs**
| Run | What happens |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | The first ring. Fifty thousand people and no one knows the choreography; it takes the whole waking day and closes at dawn with the ring lit as the sun rises. That dawn is the founding memory (rule 5). |
| Year 10 | Ten rings; the Circle is universal and has spread to homes; the Pull has chants and heroes. Risk: contractors offer to set sections early for gas (rule 9): the law of the Raising is that no section is set before the day. |
| Year 50 | Fifty rings; the first reshuffle of ward services (the cutters become carriers): a generation of complaint, then pride. Children who carried in Year 1 now set. |
| Year 300 | Three hundred rings, out to four hundred paces. The founders' ring is innermost, their number cut in it. The settlement's ground plan is its own history. Every elder can walk to the ring they set in their fiftieth year. |
| A worker's day | Hour 1: the Circle. Work. Hour 13: the Circle. Dark hours: the Pull's practice three nights a week, or the street. Sleep. |
| The bad year | An injury on the Raising: the design's real risk. Mitigation is structural: sections are small and pre-dressed, sledges not lifts, and the elders' sign-off is a real inspection, not a courtesy. A year the ring cannot be closed by dark: it closes in the next light; the festival lasts a day longer, as Bastar's often does. |
| Council objection | "Where does it end?" Answer: it doesn't; that is the point of a people with 300-year lives, and a pace a year stays inside the town for centuries. "Two minutes a shift is 2,800 hours per worker over a 200-year working life, nearly a hundred million hours across the planet." Answer: it is the cheapest thing in the culture, and the Soviets proved that taking shared time away costs more. |

**Problems and failure modes:** safety; a monument that becomes a monument to the Council (Hornbill's staging problem) unless the Raising is owned by the wards; ward services hardening into castes (the hereditary task map is exactly how caste starts; the 50-year reshuffle is not optional); the Pull favouring the strong. **Shortfalls:** no sky, no Head, little night culture; the entertainment is physical and will not suit everyone; hauling a big object to a chant is Puri's image to any judge who has seen Rath Yatra; a rope race and a work huddle are Earth staples, and only the rings are new. **Advantages:** uses the case's most pointed sentence (they *choose* work) as the engine; a very clear slide (a plan view of rings around a hall, one lit); ritual and festival are the same gesture at two scales; the Circle reaches every adult twice a day without any logistics; the rings are a history you can walk across.

**Indian references:** Bastar Dussehra (duties by village, a chariot rebuilt yearly, 75 days), Rath Yatra (the rope and the crowd pulling), Vallam Kali and Wangala's hundred drums (synchrony at scale), Durga Puja's pandals (neighbourhoods build), Dahi Handi (a trust structure); the kibbutz Omer rite and Findhorn's circle (work ritualised; not Indian). **Copy-risk: medium.** Pulling a large object through a crowd on a rope is Rath Yatra's image; a ring lit once at dusk may recall Karthigai Deepam's lamps to a Tamil judge. Defences: nothing is pulled through the town, sections are set in place; the only light is a channel of the year's gas, lit once; the growing ground-plan is no one's.

**Scorecard:** creative 4 · authentic 5 · structured 5 (14). **Headline:** "They fill their lives with work. So once a year they lay their history on the ground, one ring further out."

## 4. Two wildcards (shorter)

**W1: THE DAY OF NO PRICE (a culture about money).** Festival: one 30-hour day when nothing may be sold and everything must be given (Burning Man's decommodification; langar's free kitchen), the gas vaults sealed, and at its end every household releases one fixed measure of gas into the city's public glass "Veins" so the settlement glows for the dark hours in the mixed colours of what everyone gave, anonymously (no one knows whose light is whose; secret charity). Ritual: the Dark Hour (as in Design 4). Entertainment: the Wager, a once-a-moon prediction game on the sky and the weather, staked in a capped measure of gas, whose whole pot is released as light on the Day of No Price (Diwali-night card games are the Indian precedent for licensed, bounded gambling). Why it is a wildcard: the strongest use of fact 4 and the clearest fiscal mechanism (a yearly reset of the capitalist year, like Diwali's account books), but a city glowing at night is the single highest Diwali-recognition risk in this document, and a wager game splits judges.

**W2: THE LONG DARK (a culture about stopping).** Festival: once a year the whole settlement stops for one full 30-hour day: no light, no work, no trade, no travel, everyone at home with whoever they live with, the only sound the Watch calling the hours (Nyepi, which closes Bali's airport and is policed by neighbours; Uposatha; the Sabbath mechanism). Ritual: the synchronised sleep, all 50,000 asleep for the same ten hours every day, so the city has a true night. Entertainment: dream-telling at the first light. Why it is a wildcard: it is the purest inversion of a society that fills every hour with work, and the cheapest festival possible, but a day of enforced emptiness for lonely people can deepen the loneliness it is meant to cure (the Soviet letter again); it works only as a counterpoint inside a fuller design, which is why the Still (Design 1) and the Dark Hour (Design 4) carry its idea in smaller doses.

## 6. Recommendation

**Primary pick: Design 3, The Fifty Nights.** Reasons, in order:
