# U3. Cricket for the trade: has it been done, and did it work? (verified 8 Nov 2026)
Research note for the replacement-decision analysis of the Apollo Round 3 case. Feeds `..\Replacement_Decision\`.

**Question.** Has any brand used a sports sponsorship, cricket first, to reward its channel (dealers, retailers, mechanics, masons, painters, electricians, input retailers) rather than consumers? What was given, how was it tied to sales, and did it work?

**Method.** 41 web searches and about 45 page or PDF reads, 8 November 2026. Apollo's call transcripts (Q3 FY26 to Q2 FY27) were downloaded from corporate.apollotyres.com and searched in full. All sources were fetched on 8 November 2026; "search result only" means the page refused the fetch and only the search engine's text was seen. Press articles are paraphrased; short verbatim quotes are kept mainly for company documents, contest terms, official notices and abstracts.

**Already known (not redone):** the Champions Offer (car and two-wheeler only), "has boosted dealer morale", BKT's IPL dealer activations, Castrol's 1.27 lakh mechanics, Bain's matched-control case. See M06 / B06 / Q7 / Q5.

## 7. Synthesis [Interpretation]

**(a) Has it been done in Indian tyres, and in farm tyres?** In Indian tyres, only in light, unmeasured forms: Apollo routes consumer cricket prizes through dealers and claims higher dealer morale; BKT lists "dealer activations" without detail; CEAT holds an IPL ticket pool but publishes no dealer use. In farm tyres, no precedent was found anywhere: no tyre maker, Indian or foreign, has published a sports-linked reward for farm-tyre dealers, tractor mechanics or puncture shops. Abroad, tyre makers have done it since at least 2004, and Bridgestone writes tickets, hospitality and athlete access into its dealer programme as rewards unlocked by milestones.

**(b) Formats that recur, and which have evidence.**

| # | Format | Examples | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hospitality or trip for dealers hitting a volume target or topping a ranking | Maxxis, Linglong, Bridgestone 2023, Continental 2008 | Participation only; rankings lose the losers (section 5, rows 1 and 2) |
| 2 | Meet-the-player events for dealers and painters | Asian Paints, Nippon, Shalimar | None; no sales qualification, so not evaluable |
| 3 | Consumer bag or tyre slabs through the dealer | India Cements 2018 and 2023; Champions Offer | Response only (9,000 entries for 120 places) |
| 4 | Mechanics taken to matches | Gulf Oil | Company claim |
| 5 | Sales points for counter staff plus a sports trip draw | Firestone | Uncontrolled 30% to 50% growth claim |
| 6 | Sponsorship-themed training, top performers rewarded | Bridgestone 2016; Gulf (Q5) | None |
| 7 | Team merchandise; pass-through marks for dealers' own promotions | Shalimar, Apollo; Bridgestone, Continental | None |

Only format 5 reports a sales result, and it is uncontrolled. The research supports two design choices: tangible rewards over cash when contests repeat, and slabs many can reach rather than one ranking.

**(c) Where Apollo would be first, and what to avoid.** On the public record Apollo would be first to (1) use national-team rights, not an IPL franchise, for a sales-linked trade programme in Indian tyres; (2) aim it at farm-tyre dealers, tractor mechanics and puncture shops; (3) measure it against a control. The precedents say avoid:
1. Purchase-linked lucky draws: use skill questions and assured rewards, and check each state.
2. Ranking-only contests: JK Tyre's points scheme engaged 35% of members (Q5), and losers quit under cash prizes. Use reachable slabs and tangible rewards.
3. Events with no sales qualification: goodwill that cannot be measured.
4. Treating tickets as free: sponsor seats are scarce, contested and resellable, and trips or prizes above Rs 20,000 a year per dealer attract 10% TDS.
5. Assuming every dealer is a fan: leverage worked only among supporters, so offer cricket rewards alongside margin, not instead of it.
6. Leaving farm and truck tyres out again.
