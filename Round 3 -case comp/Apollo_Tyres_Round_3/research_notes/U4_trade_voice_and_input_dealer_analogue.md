# U4. The trade's side of the replacement choice: dealers, mechanics, tractor dealers and the farm-input analogue (verified 8 Nov 2026)
Research note for the replacement-decision analysis of the Apollo Round 3 case. Feeds `..\Replacement_Decision\`.

**Scope and method.** The trade side only; owner voice and consumer studies are in a companion note. 40 web searches (the full allowance) and about 33 page fetches on 8 November 2026; ten PDFs downloaded and read in full text. No browser used. Cited, not repeated: S5 sections 1 to 3 and B05 section 2 (channel structure, margins), Q6 sections 1 and 2 (complaints, dealer side), Q5 (adviser programmes outside tyres), B (national advice shares). Labels as in S5; "f. 8 Nov 2026" means fetched that day; "search summary" means seen only in a search digest.

**Headline.** No tyre maker, dealer, broker or rating agency has published who chooses a tractor replacement tyre. The trade evidence is indirect: dealers are multi-brand and makers compete for them with discounts; in trucking the owner-driver is the most dealer-led buyer; a Gurgaon-based tyre maker already pays puncture repairers per sale. The farm-input evidence is direct but small-sample: the retailer's word ranks first in three of six district studies on record, and credit and stock-outs are when the shop decides. [Interpretation]

## 7. Synthesis [Interpretation]

**(a) Who decides.** No trade source answers directly; this is inference.
- *Planned replacement.* The farmer has time, a fitted-brand anchor and neighbours' views; the dealer is multi-brand, paid to steer, and controls stock and credit. The likely pattern is shared: the farmer brings one or two brands, the dealer settles it on stock, price and credit. Even in pesticides no study shows most farmers simply buying what the dealer says (Rajkot: experience 45.83%, recommendation 37.5%), and for tractors advisers' recommendations rank sixth of 13. A planned tyre purchase sits between: dealer-influenced, owner-led.
- *Urgent replacement.* With the tractor idle mid-season, the choice narrows to the nearest shop with the size, the free fitter and the dealer who gives credit. The 92.5% pesticide switch and fertiliser tagging show how far the channel decides when the buyer cannot wait. Here the dealer or mechanic is close to the decider.
- *For Apollo at about 9%:* in planned purchases it loses where it is not on the shortlist; in urgent ones wherever it is not in stock (about 170 listed dealers against about 3,690 for MRF, Q4). Cricket can work on the shortlist; only the trade wins the close.

**(b) What moves a dealer or mechanic,** in order of evidence: (1) money per unit, discounts and credit notes (CCI), high-margin push (pesticides), discount-led imports (trucks); (2) credit both ways, since maker credit funds the dealer's credit to farmers, the strongest input-side lever; (3) stock and supply assurance, as dealers sell what they can get; (4) claim support, since unregistered warranties and unpaid claims recur (Q6) and CEAT lets dealers settle on the spot (S5); (5) recognition, events and trips, used by Syngenta, Dhanuka and Apollo's car-tyre trips (S5), effect unmeasured; (6) per-sale micro-rewards to fitters (3.1), effect unpublished; (7) the maker's own field staff, ranked above the retailer by farmers in one study (5.4). A trade programme supplements field presence; it does not replace it.

**(c) The analogue.** Strong on structure: the same small-town shops, credit, a knowledge gap, multi-brand shelves. It breaks on: *frequency* (pesticides several times a season, a rear tyre every few years); *ticket size* (Rs 20,000 to 30,000 a pair, M05, against a few hundred rupees, so more search); *observability* (tyre wear is attributable, pesticide efficacy is not); *the fitted brand* (no input-side OE anchor); *fitting and claims* (roles no input retailer has); *stocking cost* (fewer sizes held, so more stock-outs); *fakes and subsidy* (the pesticide and fertiliser pushes lean on both). Net: the analogue supports "the trade matters, mainly through credit and stock" but overstates the dealer's power over a planned rear pair. Planned tyre buying looks like tractor buying; urgent buying looks like pesticides. The settling test: at sample farm-tyre counters, record whether each buyer named a brand before the dealer spoke, and whether the purchase was planned or urgent.

## 9. Cautions and discrepancies

1. **Q5 mis-cites a study.** Q5 section 2 attributes the Western Uttar Pradesh study (130 dealers) to masujournal.org, 2025; it is Rai and Bhat, AJAEES 44(4), 2026. The 2025 masujournal paper is the Tamil Nadu study. Correct Q5.
2. **Rai and Bhat are inconsistent:** 200 farmers in the abstract, 400 in the methods; scores do not divide (2,500 total, mean 25.5); the first author is an agri-input marketing manager. Use the ranking only.
3. **Garrett ranks are not shares.** "Ranked first" never means a stated percentage bought the dealer's brand; no study read gives that figure.
4. **Slips:** Kannan et al. give 42.44% and 10.65% in the abstract, 43.24% and 10.58% in the table; Yaswanth et al. call the 13.75% cash buyers "credit" in the text.
5. **Small, local samples** (80 to 132 farmers, one or two districts, university work): direction, not magnitude.
6. **The CCI lines are 2012, from a dismissed case** about 2002 to 2008.
7. **CEAT's "50%"** may describe passenger tyres only.
8. **Vendor material** (Autobei, Ken Research, Xoxoday) has no published method; the Apollo match in 3.1 rests on two descriptors other Gurgaon tyre firms might share.
9. **Search summaries** carry rows 2.2, 2.3, 2.6, 3.4, 4.3 and parts of 1.8, 3.6, 5.6, 5.7, 5.9 and 5.10; the page behind 2.3 is withdrawn.
10. **Analogue buyers differ:** truck evidence concerns fleets and owner-drivers, car evidence motorists; Public Eye is a campaigning NGO; the Reuters study is unnamed.
11. **Refused or missing pages:** ndtvprofit.com, angelone.in (410), jdpower.com (403), agropages.com (403), financialfilings.com (API key), retreadingbusiness.com (404), a krishikosh thesis page.
