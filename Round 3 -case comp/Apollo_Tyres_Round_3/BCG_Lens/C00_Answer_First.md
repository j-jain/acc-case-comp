**What this folder is.** The same case, re-worked a third time, in the manner The Boston Consulting Group is publicly known to work: start from the portfolio and the environment (which kind of business is this, and should Apollo be in it at all?), apply BCG's published rules and then test whether they still apply, de-average the business into parts that need different approaches, measure value as shareholder return and its drivers, and score the odds of delivery. It is our own analysis using frameworks, findings and cases BCG has published, each cited by page and date in `C11_Sources_and_Factcheck.md`. **It is not BCG's work, and BCG has had no part in it.** BCG has published one study of the tyre industry (February 2026, global, shareholder returns) and nothing on Indian tractor owners, farm tyres, tyre dealers or cricket. The McKinsey-lens (`..\McKinsey_Lens\`) and Bain-lens (`..\Bain_Lens\`) re-analyses and the eighteen original files are unchanged apart from a dated pointer note; `C10_BCG_vs_Bain_vs_McKinsey.md` sets the three side by side.

## 1. The answer

> **Farm is a question mark in Apollo's portfolio, and Apollo has been harvesting it by omission. Decide to invest, but only where Apollo can lead: the first replacement on its own fitted tractors and the haulier whose failure every rival's warranty excludes. Win the counter on time and on registration, which no farm-tyre maker in India competes on: the tyre in the shop within 48 hours, the claim settled in seven days, every buyer registered by the dealer with "Team India's sponsor" on the card. Build the promise per occasion, so the cover goes only where hauliers are. Run the counter as a plan and the jersey's moments as experiments with controls. Fund it as capex-free, ROCE-accretive growth and the test of a rural replacement playbook, not as a share-price lever: about Rs 14 crore to March 2028, mostly reallocated inside Apollo's Rs 632 crore of trade accruals, breaking even at about 1.3 points of share.**

## 2. Three reasons

### 2.1 Farm is a question mark, and BCG's rule says concentrate or cash out

- Henderson's 1970 essay: a question mark "require[s] large added cash investment for market share to be purchased" and "is a liability unless it becomes a leader". His 1976 rule: "Any competitor with less than one quarter the share of the largest competitor cannot be an effective competitor"; "if you cannot be a leader in a product market sector, cash out as soon as practical"; "Reinvest in products and markets where you can be a successful leader." "Concentrate." The 2012 and 2014 revisits add that turbulent markets call for adaptive strategies and that companies should "invest in more question marks" as experiments. [BCG-published]
- Apollo cannot win the whole farm replacement market on cost: raw materials are 61.5% of consolidated revenue (68.3% standalone) in Q2 FY27, MRF has the volume, and BKT's margin edge is cost per kilogram, not price. The 2013 revisit of the experience curve allows what Apollo already does: "experience can be sourced externally", which is the off-take. [Verified fact; Computed; BCG-published]
- The two sectors Apollo can lead are defined by things rivals cannot copy in a season: a factory fitment (Mahindra, 43.6% of tractor sales, has fitted Apollo for two decades) and a warranty clause (Goodyear excludes trolley haulage in writing; MRF rejects it as "unequal contact pressure"). [Fact - single source; Verified fact]
- Detail: `C02`, `C03`.

### 2.2 The counter is won on occasion, time and registration, and nobody competes on any of them

- Seven demand spaces, from "my own field" to "a trolley for money", wear tyres differently; Apollo sells FX 222 for "Deep Puddling", Virat 23 for "Hard Soil" and Virat as "an all-rounder", and in 2026 no Indian maker offers any hours or occasion-specific guarantee. The cover for cuts, bursts and trolley wear goes only to tyres registered with the load at a camp, which is cheaper than one cover for all. [Verified fact; Strategic hypothesis]
- Mahindra promises "Parts Delivery available in 48 hours" and served 200,000 doorstep customers in a year; MRF promises "an intimation regarding the inspection" within seven days and keeps the decision with its inspectors; one recorded farm claim took two months. Stalk's 1988 Perspective: "Most of the time a product or service is in your value-delivery system is spent waiting." Apollo's two clocks, stock in 48 hours and settlement in seven days, are at least the "two to three times" faster he said wins. [Verified fact; BCG-published]
- BCG's sport work says value comes from "known and contactable fans" and "relationship depth", not reach; conversion runs "from as low as 1% to more than 60%". Apollo's farm business registers warranties by phone or in a dealer app and publishes no count; Hero enrolled 45 million buyers by having the dealer do it at sale. Half of farm-tyre buyers registered in the test, three in five at scale. [BCG-published; Verified fact; Strategic hypothesis]
- The haulier is found where he stands still: the sugar mill gate (more than two-thirds of cane moves by tractor trolley; the mills in five states had set up nearly 700 purchase centres; the mill fixes the load) and the 44,573 custom-hiring centres; the first replacement at the tractor dealer's service visit, where no tractor maker sells a replacement tyre on any public page. [Verified fact]
- Detail: `C04`, `C05`, `C06`.

### 2.3 It cannot move the share price, it is the cheapest growth Apollo has, and its odds can be fixed for nothing

- Apollo's five-year TSR of 14.6% a year came from revenue growth (+10.3 points) and deleveraging (+4.2); six points of farm share is 0.7% of revenue and adds about 0.17 points a year to the growth leg. A chief financial officer should refuse the programme as a TSR lever and fund it on ROCE: no plant (a farm line is Rs 430 to 790 crore for Rs 60 to 110 crore of contribution a year), working capital of a few crore, Rs 18 crore of contribution in the base case in FY29 on about Rs 15 crore of spend, and a counter, a clock and a registered base that rural car and two-wheeler replacement can reuse. [Computed; Interpretation]
- The money is mostly a reallocation: the trade and promise lines sit inside Rs 632 crore of accrued "incentives, discounts and warranty" (3.2% of standalone sales); about Rs 3 crore is new money for measurement, the office and one hospitality experiment. [Verified fact; Computed]
- On BCG's DICE scale the programme as designed scores 15 to 17; four-week learning milestones, the India business head's visible commitment with the "D", and a one-scan counter take it to about 10, "set up for success". The one factor worth four points, senior commitment, costs nothing and is the hardest to get. [BCG-published; Interpretation]
- Detail: `C07`, `C08`.

Read it plainly:

- **The BCG-lens programme sits between the two earlier ones: about Rs 2.7 crore more than the McKinsey lens and Rs 3.3 crore less than the Bain lens to March 2028, and about Rs 5.8 crore worse than the McKinsey lens at March 2029 on identical share assumptions.** The difference buys the two clocks, registration, the cover where hauliers are, the mill-gate camps, one hospitality test and a two-person office.
- **Those additions must add about four-fifths of a point of share in FY29 to pay for themselves.** That is a claim; the test decides it.
- **A point of share is worth about Rs 7.2 crore of net contribution a year; the national offer breaks even at about 1.3 points**, against 1.0 in the McKinsey lens and 1.5 in the Bain lens.
- **The programme does not move Apollo's TSR and should not be presented as if it did.** It is a Rs 100 to 200 crore revenue opportunity funded as an experiment with a cash cap of about Rs 15 crore in the worst case.

**Recommendation for the deck:** keep the McKinsey-lens deck's spine, take the Bain lens's six additions (B10 section 4.1), and add the BCG lens's eight: the invest-or-harvest frame, the TSR and ROCE case for the CFO, the cover scoped to hauliers (which saves money), the two clocks, registration as the metric, the mill-gate camp inside the haulier play, the DICE fixes, and the measurement guardrails. The merged programme is about Rs 15 to 15.5 crore to March 2028, mostly reallocated, with a break-even near 1.3 points. The case for each option is in `C10` section 4.

## 6. What we do not know

The whole case still turns on one figure, that about a quarter of Apollo's farm sales are replacement, from one broker's note. Four further unknowns now matter: how fast Apollo's own farm replacement billing has grown (which decides the quadrant); what share of the Rs 632 crore trade accrual farm gets (which decides whether the ask is a reallocation); what share of covered rear tyres are hauliers' (which decides the cover's cost); and whether the India business head will give farm the visible commitment that DICE says is worth four points. The full list is in `C10` section 5.
