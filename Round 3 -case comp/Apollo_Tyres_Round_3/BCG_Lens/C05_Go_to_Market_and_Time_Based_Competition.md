# C05. The counter: go-to-market in a fragmented channel, and time as the advantage

Prepared 7 November 2026. Part of the BCG-lens re-analysis of the Apollo Round 3 case.

**What this file is.** Our own analysis using two things BCG has published: its go-to-market work for emerging markets (the capability pages; Mittal and others, 2025; Agrawal and others, 2014; Idehen and others, 2017; and BCG's India work with the Confederation of Indian Industry, 2014 and 2015) and time-based competition (Stalk, 1988; revisited by Lesser, Reeves and Goulet, 2013). It is not BCG's work. BCG sources: `..\research_notes\S2_bcg_publications_for_case.md` section 4, `S2b` section 2, `S1` rows 2.10 to 2.12. Channel evidence: `S5_rural_gtm_distributor_economics.md`; the dealer account and the four sales plays in `..\McKinsey_Lens\M05_How_to_Win.md` and `..\Bain_Lens\B05_Commercial_Excellence_Route_to_Market.md`, which this file extends and does not repeat.

**Purpose.** To say how the counter is covered and paid under BCG's published rules for a fragmented channel, where the haulier can be found, and how speed, which no farm-tyre maker in India has made a promise about, becomes the advantage.

**Labels.** [Verified fact], [Fact - single source], [Reported by press], [Outside-in; not a survey], [Computed], [Planning assumption], [Benchmark - external], [Interpretation], [Strategic hypothesis], [BCG-published]. Where nothing reliable exists: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.

## 1. The answer in six lines

1. **Apollo's channel is one tier deep, unwritten and uncounted for farm.** It sells "on a principal to principal basis to the dealers" with no distributor and no written agreement; it invoiced 6,527 dealers and distributors in FY26 (6,933 in FY25), the top ten taking 4.3% of dealer sales; it accrued Rs 632 crore for "incentives, discounts and warranty" at 31 March 2026, about 3.2% of standalone product sales; and it publishes no count of dealers who stock a farm tyre. [Verified fact; Computed]
2. **BCG's rules for a channel like this are usable as written:** build "segment-specific coverage models"; keep no more than "15% to 20%" of the portfolio with one partner (Apollo is far inside that); tie "up to 50% of overall incentives to sales targets, product mix, and market expansion"; expect field attrition "as high as 30% a year" and "challenged partner economics"; measure sales-force effectiveness "by looking at the gross profit per sale, after subtracting all selling costs"; target rural at "sub district / tehsil" level and budget for cost to serve "upto 300 bps" higher. [BCG-published]
3. **The one promise a tractor owner already lives with is 48 hours, and it is a tractor maker's, not a tyre maker's.** Mahindra: "Tractor attended within 48 hours at the doorstep"; "Parts Delivery available in 48 hours"; 200,000 doorstep customers in a year. No tyre maker publishes any clock for a farm claim; the published norm is an "intimation" of inspection within seven days, a company inspector's decision, and a pro-rata credit later; one recorded claim took two months. [Verified fact]
4. **So Apollo competes on time, as BCG's 1988 Perspective prescribes:** "Most of the time a product or service is in your value-delivery system is spent waiting." Two clocks, both measured: the farm tyre in the shop within 48 hours of the dealer's order, and the claim settled at the dealer within seven days. Against an industry with no clock, that is the "two to three times as flexible and responsive" that Stalk said wins. [BCG-published; Strategic hypothesis]
5. **The haulier is found at a place, not by a mailing list:** the sugar mill gate and its purchase centres (more than two-thirds of cane moves by tractor trolley; the mills in five states had set up nearly 700 purchase centres; the mill fixes the load), the 44,573 custom-hiring centres, and the tractor makers' dealerships (1,000 to 1,600 each for the two largest), which run doorstep vans and sell no replacement tyre on any public page. [Verified fact; Interpretation]
6. **The money is a reallocation inside the trade pool, paid per registered tyre, with the competition-law guardrail the Bain lens wrote.** Rs 632 crore of accrued trade money is twenty times the farm programme; the programme's trade spend is the part of it that farm should already be getting. [Computed; Interpretation]

Reading [Interpretation]: the channel is simple (one tier, principal to principal), thin at the farm counter (unknown stocking), uncounted (no written terms, no farm share of the Rs 632 crore), and now instrumented (SFA, sub-dealer registration, shipment tracking). The three BCG rules that bite are partner economics (a rear pair is a tenth of a rural shop's whole opening stock), incentives tied to mix and expansion (farm is the mix; new stocking dealers are the expansion), and cost to serve (300 basis points more in thin tehsils, which is why fixed layers are gated).

### 4.3 Apollo's two clocks

[Strategic hypothesis; costs are planning assumptions]

| Clock | The promise | How it is kept | What it costs | How it is measured | Why it is hard to copy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stock in 48 hours** | A farm tyre ordered by a stocking dealer in a first-wave district is in his shop within 48 hours, in the two buying seasons | Farm sizes held at the nearest depot for the 30 districts (Apollo already runs depots and "last-mile shipment tracking"); the test's 300 tonnes ring-fenced (M08 section 2); the rural vans as a relay for villages under 5,000 people | Working capital, not expense: about 8,000 tyres in rotation for the test, about Rs 6.8 crore of inventory at makers' prices, carried at about Rs 0.5 crore a year at 8%; at national scale the depot stock already exists for car and truck tyres | Days from order to delivery, from the shipment-tracking system, reported weekly by district | Farm tyres are short (S3 section 2 row 11); a rival must choose to give the farm counter priority over the tractor maker, which Goodyear and the fitment-led makers cannot |
| **Settled in 7 days** | A claim under the hours promise or the haulier cover is settled at the dealer within seven days, on the dealer's authority up to a limit (B05: Rs 5,000 a claim), with the engineer reviewing a sample and anything above | QR registration with a photo of the hour meter; the dealer app (one app, not four); settlement credit against the dealer's next invoice; an SMS to the owner at each step | Inside M07's Rs 2.5 crore a year for the programme team, registration and claims system; the dealer's authority is a control, not a cost; a reserve of 2% (hours) and 1.5% on haulier-registered sales (`C04`) | Days from deposit to settlement, per claim, from the app; the share settled within seven days | MRF's policy bars the dealer from any representation; a rival that delegates must rebuild its claims process and its trust in dealers, which takes a season |

Reading against Stalk [Interpretation]: today's farm claim is "spent waiting", for the engineer, for the docket, for the credit. A seven-day clock against an industry whose only published number is a seven-day intimation of inspection is at least the "two to three times" Stalk required; against the two-month case it is ten times. The 2013 revisit's addition, "companies now also need to be adaptive", is the test: the claim rate by demand space is read weekly and the cover narrows if it runs above reserve (B08's rule, kept).

## 5. The coverage model, under BCG's rules

The Bain lens set out three coverage tiers and four sales plays (B05 sections 3 and 4). The BCG lens keeps them and changes what each tier is paid for and how it is measured. [Strategic hypothesis; the BCG rule is cited in each row]

| Tier | Who | What changes under BCG's rules | The BCG rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Every rural Apollo dealer in a tehsil with 3,000 or more working tractors | Stock and show the hero pair; register at the counter; settle within seven days | Paid per registered extra rear tyre (Rs 400) and per registration (Rs 100, up from M07's Rs 50, because the seller-led registration is the asset, `C06`); the first two pairs on 90 days' credit, because a pair is a tenth of a rural shop's stock | "challenged partner economics (RoI, Low absolute earnings)"; incentives tied to "product mix, and market expansion" |
| 2. District farm hubs in dense tehsils | The mechanic programme, the camps, the haulier accounts, the claims review | Funded only where the field engine clears five points (M07), because rural cost to serve runs "upto 300 bps" higher; the district ARD measured on gross profit per registered sale less selling cost | "sub district / tehsil" targeting; SFE as "gross profit per sale, after subtracting all selling costs" |
| 3. Rural Mobile Stores (25 vans) | Villages under 5,000 people; pressure checks; registration; orders for the nearest dealer | A relay for the 48-hour promise where no dealer is within reach; no farm stock on the van beyond fronts | "mixing and matching is the most effective way to achieve target coverage" |
| 4. Tractor makers' dealerships | The first-replacement list; the returning-owner card | The service visit (Mahindra's 200,000 doorstep calls a year) as the registration point for Apollo-fitted tractors; a replacement tyre offered where no tractor maker sells one today | "more than 80% of sales coming through either direct or tier-one distribution partners" |
| 5. Aggregators: mill gates, custom-hiring centres, FPOs and PACS | The haulier accounts; bulk orders before the season | New: the mill-gate camp (section 6) | "packages specifically compiled for fleets" (the European truck finding, transferred) |

Two rules on the field force [Interpretation; the evidence is S5 section 3 and BCG's attrition line]:

- **Pay the rural officer on registered farm sales and days-to-deliver, not on calls.** Rural field staff in India leave over incentives more than salary (a staffing firm's FMCG report), and BCG warns of attrition "as high as 30% a year". The sales-force automation Apollo rolled out in FY26 can measure both numbers.
- **Keep the farm team inside the rural sales organisation,** as M07 section 7.2 argued, because the same officer carries car and two-wheeler tyres to the same counter; BCG's line that companies "absorb the cost of the channel partner's sales team" is the expensive alternative.

## 6. The haulier channel: where the operator stands still

### 6.1 The mill gate

The government-commissioned sugarcane transport study (AERC, University of Delhi, February 2021; 1,800 farmers in 115 villages of eight states; 29 mills; the 2018-19 season): "The tractor trolley is the most common mode of transportation as nearly more than two-thirds of the sugarcane produce in the study states is transported by this source. Of the total quantity sold, 91% is transported using the mechanized mode." Trolley loads are fixed at the gate by state: Punjab "150-170 quintals"; western Uttar Pradesh "55-60 quintals"; Uttarakhand "80-90 quintals"; Bihar "70 to 80 quintals". in Bihar, "On an average 20 to 35 purchase centres are being operated by each sugar mill"; the mills covered in five states had established "nearly 700 purchase centres"; about 80% of cane arrives at the gate; the farmer pays the haul to the gate himself; hauliers face "long waiting time in queues at mill gates" and "trolley overturned". [Verified fact: study read; S5 section 5]

Illustration [Computed; illustrative]: a 5,000-tonne-a-day mill receiving 80% at the gate at 5.75 tonnes a trolley (western Uttar Pradesh) needs about 700 trolley arrivals a day, about 1,05,000 in a 150-day season; at 16 tonnes a trolley (Punjab) about 300 a day. Trips per tractor are unknown, so this sizes trips, not fleets. Maharashtra's cane fleet at 26 mills is "over 18,850 tractors" (an NGO's count, February 2026). [Reported by press]

**The mill-gate camp** [Strategic hypothesis; cost a planning assumption]: a pressure-and-load check, registration of the tractor and its trolley with the hour meter and the mill's load slip, the cover explained, and an order for the nearest stocking dealer, at the gate and the largest purchase centres, in the weeks before crushing starts. Cost about Rs 1 lakh a mill a season (a van, two people, boards); ten mills in the cotton belt and western Uttar Pradesh in the first wave (about Rs 10 lakh); Maharashtra in the second. The mill itself is the aggregator BCG's fleet work describes: it fixes the load, it runs the centres, and it has reason to want trolleys that do not overturn in its queue.

### 6.2 The custom-hiring centre

44,573 custom-hiring centres, hi-tech hubs and farm machinery banks were established from 2014-15 to 2023-24 (Uttar Pradesh 8,644; Haryana 2,313; Madhya Pradesh 1,446; Maharashtra 1,382; Rajasthan 1,363; Punjab 1,256). Seven cooperative centres in south-western Punjab run "an average annual usage of 900 hours". [Verified fact: PIB annexure; Fact - single source] They are the organised fleet: a known address, a machinery register, hours in the hundreds, and tyres bought by a manager. BCG's 2021 line that "only 15% to 30% of registered users are active" is the planning rule for the share of centres that will buy in a season. [BCG-published; Interpretation]

### 6.3 The tractor maker's dealership

Mahindra lists "1000+ Authorized Dealership & 300+ Authorized Service Centers" and 200,000 doorstep service customers a year; TAFE "over 1600+ dealers" and 1,500 authorised workshops. No tractor maker's public page sells or prices a replacement tyre, and no OEM-branded tyre or OEM-and-tyre-maker replacement tie-up was found. [Verified fact: company pages; Reported by press; S5 section 6] For the first-replacement play that is the opening: the service visit at years three to five is the moment the tyre on an Apollo-fitted tractor is seen, and nobody sells one there. Whether that is policy or oversight is the first question for the key-account head (B05 Play 2). [Interpretation]

## 7. The money and the guardrail

### 7.1 Where the trade money comes from

| Line | Figure | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Apollo's accrued incentives, discounts and warranty, 31 March 2026 | Rs 632.3 crore, about 3.2% of standalone product sales | [Verified fact; Computed] |
| Farm's pro-rata share at 6% of domestic revenue | About Rs 38 crore a year; the actual farm share is not public | [Computed; Planning assumption] |
| The programme's trade and promise cost at national scale (M07's national offer plus the haulier cover and the registration bonus) | About Rs 8 crore a year fixed; Rs 1,255 to 1,475 a rear tyre variable, paid only on extra registered tyres | [Computed; `C07`] |
| The reading | The fixed cost is a fifth of farm's pro-rata share of the pool, and the variable cost is paid only on tyres that would not otherwise have been sold | [Interpretation] |

The Bain lens funded its programme from the farm share of advertising and promotion (B07 section 4). The BCG lens funds the trade and promise part from the trade pool, where it belongs, and leaves the measurement, the transformation office and the experiments as the only new money (`C07`). [Strategic hypothesis]

### 7.2 The competition-law guardrail

Kept as the Bain lens wrote it (B05 section 5; B08 section 4): no minimum resale price, no tie-in of tubes or fronts to the guaranteed rear, no exclusivity condition on the dealer, no exchange of scheme terms with rivals, and written clearance before the first rupee. The Competition Commission's 2012 record of this trade and its 2018 penalty of Rs 425.53 crore on Apollo (appeals dismissed by the Supreme Court in January 2022; penalty remitted for review that December) are the reason. The 48-hour stock promise and the seven-day settlement are service terms, not price terms, and pass; a "stock only Apollo farm" condition would not. [Verified fact; Interpretation]

**Status of the speed claim in the Day-1 answer** ("win it on speed"). Supported, and sharpened: the Day-1 answer assumed speed was one advantage among several; the research shows it is the one dimension on which no farm-tyre maker in India has made any promise at all, while the tractor makers the owner deals with have made a 48-hour one.
