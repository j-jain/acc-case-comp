Prepared 6 Nov 2026 for Apollo Tyres x ACC Round 3. Research only; no recommendations for Apollo. 37 of 38 web searches used. Items marked (INT) are my interpretation, not a source claim.

## 1. Sports sponsorship ROI and activation

### Takeaway
Published evidence says sponsors typically under-fund activation relative to rights, that a minority measure ROI properly, and that sponsorship reliably moves awareness and purchase intent but rarely has audited sales proof. The Indian jersey record shows brand-visibility wins and business failures driven by company or regulatory events; no audited awareness-to-sales evidence exists for any jersey sponsor.

### Entries

**E1. McKinsey, "Is sports sponsorship worth it?" (Jeff Jacobs, 2014)** [Single source; McKinsey page not opened, read via Roaring Fork Agency summary of 8 Dec 2014 and search snippets; US data, 12 years old]
1. Problem: sponsors pay large rights fees and cannot show return.
2. Approach: five-metric programme: cost per reach; unaided awareness per reach; sales or margin per dollar (two-step model or econometrics); long-term brand attributes; indirect benefits.
3. Results: one-third to one-half of US companies lack a comprehensive ROI system (industry research cited by McKinsey, 2013 context; US sponsorship about USD 20bn). Activation spend ran USD 0.50 to 1.60 per USD 1 of rights (McKinsey experience plus IEG 2011). A CPG firm split 80% rights, 20% activation; shifting money to activation lifted unaided awareness of its top sponsorships 15%. A handset maker found a 10x ROI gap between top and bottom quartile sponsorships. McKinsey says comprehensive measurement can raise returns "up to 30%" (a consultancy claim, not a study).
4. (INT) Transfers: the discipline of reporting cost per reach and unaided awareness among the target group, not total audience; the idea that rights are a fraction of total cost.
5. (INT) Do not copy: US ratios and the 30% claim as a benchmark for India; Rs 579 cr rights with a 0.5-1.6x activation ratio would imply a very large activation budget, which a case on "affordable" must test rather than assume.
6. (INT) Adaptation: define "reach" as farmer and landowner reach by district, and unaided recall as the primary metric; use a two-step link to dealer enquiries and sales.
Note: an activation ratio of USD 1.50-3.00 per USD 1 of rights circulates on agency blogs; it is not in the McKinsey text I could verify [Unverified secondary claim].

**E2. Nielsen Sports, "Sports sponsorships are raising more than just brand awareness" (March 2022)** [Single source, primary but unaudited]
1. Problem: whether sponsorship does more than build awareness.
2. Approach: analysis of 100 sponsorships, 2020-21, seven markets, 20 industries (method, sample sizes and controls not disclosed).
3. Results: average 10% lift in purchase intent among exposed fans; purchase intent rose more than familiarity in 13 industries; Nielsen "experience base" says a 1-point gain in awareness or consideration drives about 1% sales.
4. (INT) Transfers: supports measuring consideration and intent, not just recall.
5. (INT) Do not copy: it measures intent, not sales; pandemic period; mostly not rural or durable goods. The 1:1 awareness-to-sales rule is unsupported by shown data.
6. (INT) Adaptation: treat as an upper-bound sanity check; require a district-level control group before claiming sales effect.
Academic counterpoint (search snippet, Sport Management Review): sponsor image, not awareness alone, mediates purchase intent [Single source].

**E3. GroupM ESP "Sporting Nation" series (size of Indian sports marketing and cricket share)** [Single source per figure; trade press relays, definitions and year labels inconsistent]
1. Problem: size and shape of the Indian sponsorship market.
2. Approach: annual ESP Properties (GroupM) market sizing.
3. Results: cricket 87% of sponsorship in 2020 (Rs 5,133 cr of Rs 5,894 cr); sports industry crossed Rs 14,000 cr in 2022 (+49% on 2021); about Rs 15,000 cr in 2023 with cricket about 85-87% (Rs 13,701 cr in one report); the 12th edition reports a market above Rs 16,633 cr with 6% growth and emerging sports about 15% share. A narrower sponsorship-only figure (Rs 7,345 cr, +24% in 2023) also appears. Cricket's exact 2024 share was not confirmed.
4. (INT) Transfers: cricket is the dominant, crowded property; a Rs 579 cr deal is a few per cent of national sports marketing spend by any definition.
5. (INT) Do not copy: do not mix the "sports industry" (media, endorsement, sponsorship) and "sponsorship only" totals when sizing.
6. (INT) Adaptation: use as context for share-of-voice, with the primary ESP PDF cited before use.
Sources: register items 4 to 7.

**E4. Team India jersey sponsors: Sahara, Oppo, Byju's, Dream11, then Apollo** [Verified fact for the deal facts; outcomes Single source]
1. Problem: did the most expensive Indian sponsorship property pay back.
2. Approach: BCCI lead-sponsor rights, bidding cycles.
3. Results: Oppo 2017-19, claimed 80%+ aided awareness (one brand commentator [Unverified secondary claim]); Byju's exited early (reported Rs 1,079 cr deal; other reports differ); Dream11 signed Mar 2023 at Rs 358 cr, exited Aug 2025 after India's real-money-gaming ban. Apollo: reported Rs 579 cr (not officially disclosed), 16 Sep 2025, to March 2028, 121 bilateral + 21 ICC matches, reported Rs 4.77 cr per match; rival bids Canva Rs 544 cr, JK Cement Rs 477 cr. Failure causes in press are specific (Sahara collapse, Byju's over-extension, Dream11 regulation), not a "curse". No audited awareness-to-sales figure for any jersey sponsor: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: the property is a low-risk visibility asset for a solvent, unregulated company; risk of early exit is mostly company-specific.
5. (INT) Do not copy: Oppo and Dream11 sold to urban or digital, high-frequency buyers; none was a rural, high-ticket, dealer-led purchase.
6. (INT) Adaptation: measure Apollo's own farm-segment recall before and after, since no sponsor precedent exists.
Apollo's stated aims (Storyboard18, 16 Sep 2025): national pride, consumer trust, category leadership; no rural or farm statement in that piece.

## 2. Marketing-effectiveness frameworks

### Takeaway
The frameworks agree that brand-building and activation both matter and that long-run effects come from mental availability, with distribution (physical availability) as the partner. All were built on Western and mostly FMCG or services data; none is validated for rural Indian farm inputs.

**E5. Binet and Field, IPA "The Long and the Short of It" (2013)** [Verified fact as a framework]
1. Problem: how to split budget between brand building and short-term activation.
2. Approach: IPA Databank of 996 campaigns, 1980-2010.
3. Results: optimum about 60% brand, 40% activation; "Effectiveness in Context" (2018) cites 62:38; brand effects need about six months or more to overtake short-term effects. A B2B figure near 46:54 circulates but I could not verify it.
4. (INT) Transfers: a two-track plan (recall plus a conversion mechanism) rather than reliance on jersey visibility alone.
5. (INT) Do not copy: 60:40 as a rule; the database is UK effectiveness-award winners, mass media era.
6. (INT) Adaptation: use as a prompt to show a distinct budget line for both tracks.

**E6. Ehrenberg-Bass Institute / Byron Sharp: mental and physical availability** [Verified fact as a framework; secondary summaries only]
1. Problem: what drives brand growth.
2. Approach: panel data on buyer behaviour; "How Brands Grow".
3. Results: growth comes from reach and being easy to buy; "Double Jeopardy" (smaller brands have fewer and slightly less loyal buyers); reach beats narrow targeting; distinctive assets build memory. A 2022 Ehrenberg-Bass B2B paper extends the argument.
4. (INT) Transfers: a goal of being "first thought of" is mental availability; the link to sales needs physical availability (a dealer who stocks and fits the tyre).
5. (INT) Do not copy: FMCG-trained claims about reach and promotions on a durable bought rarely, via a dealer.
6. (INT) Adaptation: define both measures by district: share of farmers who name Apollo first, and share of dealers who stock it.

**E7. McKinsey "The Consumer Decision Journey" (David Court et al., 2009)** [Single source via summaries; 2008-09 data, 20,000 decisions, five industries, three continents]
1. Problem: the funnel misdescribes how people buy.
2. Approach: survey of purchase decisions, circular journey with a loyalty loop.
3. Results: roughly two-thirds of touchpoints in active evaluation are consumer-driven (word of mouth, reviews). Brands in the initial consideration set are far more likely to be bought, but the "three times" figure is from a secondary summary [Unverified secondary claim].
4. (INT) Transfers: peer and mechanic voice matters most late in the journey, which fits a farm-tyre purchase.
5. (INT) Do not copy: consumer-goods and Western touchpoints.
6. (INT) Adaptation: map the farmer journey from first thought to fit-and-pay, placing cricket at the first stage only.

**E8. Geo-experiments and matched-market tests** [Unverified secondary claim; only vendor blogs found]
1. Problem: proving incremental sales from media.
2. Approach: vary exposure by region, compare outcomes (Google Geo Experiments / Matched Markets / Meridian GeoX; Meta GeoLift is open source, not a native feature).
3. Results: no published effect sizes for India; vendor sources say matched markets suffer leakage so synthetic controls are preferred. Official Google or Meta design guidance: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: the right design for a "measurable" claim at district level.
5. (INT) Do not copy: online-conversion assumptions; farm-tyre sales are offline and slow.
6. (INT) Adaptation: pick matched districts by tractor population and dealer count; track dealer enquiries and invoices.

## 3. Rural India consumer and go-to-market reports

### Takeaway
Rural consumption is outpacing urban in recent FMCG panels, and rural shoppers are digitising, but offline channels remain central. Most consulting rural reports found are FMCG or e-commerce focused and some are old.

**E9. Accenture "Masters of Rural Markets" (2010-2015 editions)** [Single source, 2015, dated]
1. Problem: why firms struggle to reach, acquire and retain rural customers.
2. Approach: third edition "From Touchpoints to Trustpoints" (Jan 2015) surveyed 2,800 consumers and customer service officers at 40 businesses; four rural segments.
3. Results: loyalty and trust initiatives improve rural competitiveness; firms still lean on conventional channels such as celebrity ads.
4. (INT) Transfers: the reach-acquire-retain frame, and trust as the scarce asset.
5. (INT) Do not copy: 2015 numbers and FMCG and financial-services focus.
6. (INT) Adaptation: add a fourth stage, "fit and service", for a tyre.

**E10. Kantar-GroupM Rural Barometer (2024) and NielsenIQ** [Single source each; measures differ and conflict]
1. Problem: how fast is rural growing and how is it changing.
2. Approach: household panels.
3. Results: average rural FMCG basket 5.88 items (2022) to 9.3 (2024); NielsenIQ Q3 2024 rural value growth 6.7% vs urban 5.0% (another report: 6.0% vs 2.8%; unresolved conflict); Kantar Worldpanel: small towns and villages 42% of premium FMCG sales vs 30% four years earlier. Campaign India headline says 47% of rural India embraces digital alongside traditional media (figure not verified in text).
4. (INT) Transfers: rural media habits are hybrid, so cricket on TV and mobile reaches rural audiences.
5. (INT) Do not copy: FMCG basket data as a signal for tractor-tyre demand.
6. (INT) Adaptation: use as media-mix context only.

**E11. BCG on rural digital and online shopping** [Single source, press relays]
1. Problem: how digital rural India is becoming.
2. Approach: 2026 "$300 Billion Connected Commerce" survey of 12,000+ consumers; 2022 projection.
3. Results: about 300m online shoppers, about 30% rural; offline retail still grows 13-14% a year; 2022 forecast rural 54% of shoppers by 2030 [Forecast]. Older BCG rural survey (c.2016): 4,000 consumers, 27 villages, 14 states. No Bain or CII rural report found.
4. (INT) Transfers: omnichannel, not digital-only.
5. (INT) Do not copy: consumer e-commerce metrics for farm equipment.
6. (INT) Adaptation: test a digital lead capture, dealer closing.
Tractor context: TMA domestic tractor sales about 1.09m units in 2025, +20% on 2024; ICRA expects 15-17% growth in FY2026 [Single source each]. No 2025 Deloitte, EY or KPMG mechanisation report found.

## 4. Farmer-specific research

### Takeaway
The only consultancy farmer data located for India are McKinsey's 2024 survey, which shows farmers prefer a mix of in-person and digital. Dealer-versus-online trust percentages were not accessible. Peer and demonstration influence appears in small academic studies.

**E12. McKinsey Global Farmer Insights, India view (survey early 2024, n=1,031; published 17 Jan 2025)** [Single source via Entrepreneur India and snippets; McKinsey page not opened]
1. Problem: what Indian farmers want and how they buy.
2. Approach: survey, part of a 4,400-producer global survey.
3. Results: digital payments 11% (2022) to 43% (2024), mostly in person at retail stores; crop insurance 8% to 37%; equipment insurance 4% to 23%; bank financing 9% to 36%; three-quarters cite high interest; only about 1 in 10 prefer digital at every purchase stage; biologicals 7% to 11%. McKinsey recommends combining physical and digital. Percent trusting dealers vs online: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation. A 2026 global edition (5,500 farmers, 10 markets) exists; no India split found.
4. (INT) Transfers: the in-person retail point is where farmers still transact; finance and insurance uptake suggests credit is a purchase lever.
5. (INT) Do not copy: input-purchase findings applied to a tyre without checking.
6. (INT) Adaptation: run a primary dealer-farmer survey in two districts.

**E13. Tractor buyer behaviour studies (Adilabad 2020-21; J.D. Power 2018)** [Single source each; small or old]
1. Problem: what drives tractor choice.
2. Approach: 120 farmers plus dealers (Telangana); 3,835 owners across 14 states (J.D. Power).
3. Results: fuel efficiency ranked first, then subsidy and maintenance cost; demonstrations ranked first among promotions, then word of mouth, then vans and hoardings; dealers ranked factors differently. J.D. Power: 21% considered another brand in 2018 vs 14% in 2015; younger rural cohorts less influenced by word of mouth.
4. (INT) Transfers: in-person demonstration and peers beat mass hoardings for farm buyers.
5. (INT) Do not copy: tractor purchase findings for a replacement tyre (lower ticket, mechanic-influenced; this is untested).
6. (INT) Adaptation: test whether the same ranking holds for tyres.

## 5. Indian rural go-to-market cases

### Takeaway
Programmes that put a human contact in the village (Shakti, e-Choupal, Khushiyon Ki Doli) have documented scale, but outcomes are company-reported and rarely give sales uplift per rupee.

**E14. HUL Project Shakti (from 2001)** [Single source, company-reported]
1. Problem: reach villages with no distributor.
2. Approach: women micro-entrepreneurs ("Shakti Ammas") selling door to door.
3. Results: 1,36,000+ entrepreneurs across 18 states by end 2020 (HUL page via snippet); older figure 3m households and 100,000 villages. Cost and incremental sales: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: local person as last-mile trust carrier.
5. (INT) Do not copy: it sells low-ticket FMCG with repeat purchase; tyres are infrequent.
6. (INT) Adaptation: analogue is a trusted local mechanic or dealer as the node.

**E15. ITC e-Choupal** [Single source, company and case listings, no independent evaluation]
1. Problem: farmers' price opacity and ITC's procurement cost.
2. Approach: village internet kiosks run by lead farmers.
3. Results: over 4m farmers, 31,000+ villages, 6,100 kiosks, 10 states (20-year report); about 2.5% higher price to farmers selling to ITC; one listing says ITC market share 8% to 12% (unverified).
4. (INT) Transfers: the local, trusted node also becomes a distribution channel later.
5. (INT) Do not copy: it solved a procurement problem; benefits were price information, not brand recall.
6. (INT) Adaptation: dealer hubs as information points.

**E16. HUL Khushiyon Ki Doli (March 2010)** [Single source, Campaign India 26 Aug 2010]
1. Problem: reach "media dark" villages.
2. Approach: three steps: village gatherings (TV ads, demos, games), door-to-door trial, separate retail visibility team; multi-brand to share cost.
3. Results: target 14 million consumers in 35,000 villages across three states; HUL said trials, sales and distribution increased; no figures. Cost undisclosed.
4. (INT) Transfers: pairing awareness with trial and retail availability in the same wave.
5. (INT) Do not copy: FMCG sampling.
6. (INT) Adaptation: pair a cricket moment with a village demo and dealer visit.

**E17. Hero MotoCorp "Har Gaon Har Aangan" (2008)** [Unverified secondary claim for results]
1. Problem: rural two-wheeler penetration.
2. Approach: village activation targeting 100,000 of 600,000 villages in a year.
3. Results: a student deck claims 130,000 villages; a blog claims 15-16k extra bikes per wave (no source). Business Standard (2007 column) questioned whether Hero Honda's cricket advertising protected its lead over Bajaj [Single source, opinion].
4. (INT) Transfers: village coverage as a trackable KPI.
5. (INT) Do not copy: outcome numbers.
6. (INT) Adaptation: coverage KPI by district.

**E18. Tata Ace and Mahindra Samriddhi/Krish-e** [Single source, low-reliability decks for Ace]
1. Problem: serve rural small business and farm buyers.
2. Approach: Ace mini-truck with financing, lifecycle cost message and about 600 rural distributorships (student decks). Mahindra turned dealerships into Samriddhi advice centres (first in Jamnagar 2008) and now Krish-e centres (soil testing, demonstration plots, equipment rental).
3. Results: Ace over 100,000 units in under two years (Business Today, Jul 2007). Krish-e: 15,000 smart kits (FY2022 report); 45 centres in an awards edition. Sales uplift: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: finance and total cost message for rural buyers; dealer as service hub.
5. (INT) Do not copy: Ace was a new category.
6. (INT) Adaptation: dealer-as-advice-point.

## 6. Influencer-channel programmes

### Takeaway
Mechanic, contractor and carpenter programmes publish enrolment and event counts, not sales uplift. Digital scan-and-pay is the common mechanic.

**E19. Castrol India Fast Scan and Super Mechanic** [Single source, company press release 2019]
1. Problem: lubricant choice sits with the mechanic.
2. Approach: QR in the pack cap scanned by a mechanic or retailer; money paid into the bank account; plus Super Mechanic skills contest since 2017.
3. Results: over 1.5 lakh mechanics and retailers enrolled by Sept 2019, aiming to double in 12 months. Sales uplift: Data unavailable. Gulf Oil programme: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: pay the influencer instantly via UPI; tie reward to a verifiable pack.
5. (INT) Do not copy: oil is a repeat purchase with a pack code; tyres are less frequent.
6. (INT) Adaptation: tyre serial scan.

**E20. Pidilite Fevicol Champions' Club** [Single source, old]
1. Problem: carpenters recommend adhesive brands.
2. Approach: club for woodworking contractors since 2002; points loyalty from 2012; annual Shram Daan Diwas.
3. Results: about 22,000 contractors in 145 cities repaired furniture at about 325 institutions in 2013; later source 300 clubs, 40,000+ members. Sales effect: Data unavailable.
4. (INT) Transfers: identity and community, not only points.
5. (INT) Do not copy: CSR event as a sales tool.
6. (INT) Adaptation: recognition for mechanics.

**E21. Ambuja Abhimaan (cement contractors), Asian Paints Masterstrokes, agri-input farmer programmes** [Single source each]
1. Problem: influencers steer purchase.
2. Approach: Ambuja: relationships, skills, rewards, certification. Asian Paints: points for contractors. Agri-input: demos and meetings (Dhanuka field demos; IIL 514 meetings, 25,000+ farmers; Campaign India Jan 2025 on phygital agri marketing).
3. Results: Ambuja 70,000+ contractors enrolled, award at Mint Marketing Summit 2021 (an award, not a sales result). Others: no outcomes published; UPL, Syngenta, Bayer numbers: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation. A 5m-follower farmer YouTube channel exists (Campaign India).
4. (INT) Transfers: certification and training give the influencer a reason to stay.
5. (INT) Do not copy: enrolment counts as success.
6. (INT) Adaptation: track enrolled mechanics who actually generate leads.

## 7. Cricket or sport for mass-market and rural brand building

### Takeaway
FMCG cricket cases show large claimed effects, all self-reported in award entries. Tractor and tyre brands already use cricket, but publish no results.

**E22. Cadbury Dairy Milk x IPL and Thums Up x ICC events** [Single source; self-reported award entries]
1. Problem: grow share via cricket moments.
2. Approach: IPL "Thank You" third year (2024); Thums Up T20 World Cup and "Toofani Tour".
3. Results: Cadbury sales +45%, penetration +6.9%, spontaneous awareness +3%, consideration +2% (budget not stated). Thums Up: 51m units and highest household penetration (T20 WC case); Toofani Tour +9% spontaneous awareness, +4pt association with cricket, +1% share, 100m reached.
4. (INT) Transfers: tie a cricket moment to a purchase mechanic.
5. (INT) Do not copy: fast-moving, low-ticket, self-reported, metro-leaning.
6. (INT) Adaptation: set targets in awareness points and share, not sales percent.

**E23. Direct tyre and tractor analogues: BKT, CEAT, Sonalika, Swaraj, Mahindra OJA** [Verified fact for existence; outcomes unavailable]
1. Problem: build recall for farm and tyre brands.
2. Approach: BKT (an off-highway and agricultural tyre maker) is Official Tyre Partner of 8 of 10 IPL teams for 2026, with stadium visibility, broadcast integrations, dealer activations, grassroots programmes and digital engagement (Autocar Pro, 25 Mar 2026). CEAT: 30+ years in cricket, IPL central sponsorship renewed five years (Feb 2024). Sonalika "Jeetne Ka Dum" on TATA IPL 2026 playoffs. Swaraj renewed MS Dhoni with "Bharosa" campaign. Mahindra OJA tractor ads in World Cup 2023.
3. Results: none published. Independent recall survey of CEAT vs MRF vs Apollo vs JK: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation. BKT fee: Data unavailable.
4. (INT) Transfers: competitors have already occupied farm-cricket space, so "first to think of" is contested.
5. (INT) Do not copy: press-release success without measurement.
6. (INT) Adaptation: benchmark Apollo against BKT and CEAT recall.

## 8. Last-mile distribution lessons

### Takeaway
I did not find a McKinsey, BCG or Bain route-to-market text on this; evidence is from Godrej, NielsenIQ and academic summaries.

**E24. Godrej Consumer Products micro-markets, NielsenIQ "Village Hit List"** [Single source, company-reported / product page]
1. Problem: spread of distribution cost across India's villages.
2. Approach: Godrej defines micro-markets (district clusters) per brand; added over 100,000 rural outlets (annual report FY2018-19). NielsenIQ (26 Jun 2025) sells a priority-district report; says rural is over 65% of population, method not disclosed.
3. Results: no sales outcomes available. McKinsey/BCG/Bain route-to-market: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.
4. (INT) Transfers: prioritise districts instead of spreading evenly.
5. (INT) Do not copy: FMCG outlet counts.
6. (INT) Adaptation: rank districts by tractor population and dealer coverage.
