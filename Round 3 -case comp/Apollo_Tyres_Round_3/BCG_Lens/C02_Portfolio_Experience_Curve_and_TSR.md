# C02. The portfolio, the cost position and shareholder return: invest or harvest?

Prepared 7 November 2026. Part of the BCG-lens re-analysis of the Apollo Round 3 case.

**What this file is.** Our own analysis using four things BCG has published: the growth share matrix (Henderson, 1970; revisited 2014), the experience curve (Henderson, 1968; revisited 2013), the Rule of Three and Four (Henderson, 1976; revisited 2012) and the February 2026 study of tyre makers' shareholder returns, "The Tire Industry Paradox". It is not BCG's work, and BCG has had no part in it. Every BCG quotation is from a bcg.com page or a BCG PDF read on 7 November 2026 and listed in `C11_Sources_and_Factcheck.md`; the research behind the Apollo figures is `..\research_notes\S3_portfolio_cost_position_tsr.md`, with S1 and S2 for the BCG pages. Market arithmetic is reused from `..\McKinsey_Lens\M02_Value_at_Stake.md` and `..\Bain_Lens\B02_Profit_Pools_and_Leadership_Economics.md` and is not re-derived.

**Purpose.** To answer the question a BCG team is publicly known to ask before any marketing question: where does farm sit in Apollo's portfolio, what does Apollo's cost position allow, what have tyre makers' shareholder returns actually rewarded, and so whether Apollo should invest in farm replacement at all, and if so where.

**Labels.** [Verified fact], [Fact - single source], [Reported by press], [Computed], [Planning assumption], [Interpretation], [Strategic hypothesis], [BCG-published], [Secondary on BCG]. Where nothing reliable exists: Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation.

## 1. The answer in six lines

1. **Indian farm replacement is a question mark, not a pet.** It grew 6.9% a year in units from FY19 to FY24, faster than tyre replacement as a whole, and Apollo holds perhaps a quarter to a third of the leader's share in it. Management runs it as if it were a cash cow on a fixed plant: farm is absent from the FY2026 targets, every rupee of growth capital goes to car and truck radials, the European farm line at Enschede (20 tonnes a day) has closed, and the stated medium-term plan is off-take. [Computed; Verified fact; Interpretation]
2. **BCG's own rule for that position is blunt.** Henderson, 1970: a question mark "require[s] large added cash investment for market share to be purchased" and "is a liability unless it becomes a leader". Henderson, 1976: "Any competitor with less than one quarter the share of the largest competitor cannot be an effective competitor", so "if you cannot be a leader in a product market sector, cash out as soon as practical" and "Concentrate." [BCG-published]
3. **Apollo cannot be the farm replacement leader across the whole market, and should not try.** The experience curve gives MRF the volume, and in 2026 raw materials are 61.5% of Apollo's consolidated revenue (68.3% standalone), so the cost position is set by material and conversion cost per kilogram, where BKT, not scale, shows the way. The 2013 revisit allows what Apollo is already doing: "experience can be sourced externally under certain circumstances", which is what the off-take partners are. [Verified fact; Computed; BCG-published; Interpretation]
4. **So concentrate in the sectors where Apollo can be the leader:** the first replacement on tractors that left the factory on Apollo tyres, and the haulier, whose tyre failure every rival's warranty excludes. That is the same segment choice the Bain lens reached through leadership economics; the BCG route gets there through the rule's cash-out test. [Strategic hypothesis]
5. **The CFO cannot be sold farm on its own TSR.** One point of replacement share is about Rs 33 crore of revenue, 0.12% of Apollo's consolidated revenue; the full potential of six points is 0.7% of revenue and 1 to 1.5% of EBITDA. Apollo's five-year TSR of 14.6% a year came from revenue growth (+10.3 points) and deleveraging (+4.2), nothing from margin or multiple. Farm cannot move those legs. [Computed; Interpretation]
6. **What farm can be is capex-free, ROCE-accretive growth and a test of a repeatable rural-replacement playbook.** BCG's tyre study says "sales growth remains the primary engine of TSR" and credits the industry's top returns to "Asian budget challengers from India and China"; management's own anchor is a mid-teens return on capital employed. A programme whose cost is almost all variable, which needs no plant, and whose counter, clock and registration transfer to rural car and two-wheeler replacement, is the kind of investment the portfolio can carry. [BCG-published; Verified fact; Interpretation]

Three readings [Interpretation]:

- **Farm replacement is the one Indian business where Apollo is a small follower in a market growing at or above the industry's rate.** That is Henderson's question mark exactly: it "require[s] far more cash than [it] can generate" if Apollo tries to buy share across the whole market, and it becomes a pet "when the growth stops" if Apollo merely holds share.
- **Management has already answered the invest-or-harvest question by its actions, without saying so.** The FY2026 "Vision" targets (USD 5 billion of revenue, EBITDA above 15%, ROCE of 12 to 15%, net debt below two times EBITDA) do not mention farm; the FY26 strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats list names truck radialisation, premium cars and two-wheelers and not farm; every rupee of the Rs 3,000 crore (FY27) and Rs 2,200 crore (FY28) capex is for car and truck radials; the chief financial officer said in August 2026 that a 10 to 15 tonne-a-day Indian farm plant "would not be an economic sized capacity" and that "the midterm plan is to continue with the offtake". [Verified fact: S3 sections 2, 7] Indian farm is being run as a cash cow on a fixed plant, with its replacement growth acknowledged ("Tractor Rear tyres continued to be a key driver of value") and given no capacity.
- **The 2014 revisit changes what "invest" means.** Time in a quadrant has halved; share no longer predicts performance on its own; the matrix now "requires companies to invest in more question marks" and to "Measure and manage portfolio economics of experimentation". The honest investment in a question mark of this size is a controlled experiment with a cash cap and a gate, not a plant. That is what the programme in `C07` is.

### 2.3 What the matrix does not settle

The placement rests on three figures that only Apollo holds: its farm replacement share (one broker note), its fitment share at each tractor maker, and the growth of its own farm replacement billing. Data unavailable – requires primary research or company confirmation. If Apollo's replacement share is nearer 15% than 9%, farm replacement moves toward the cash-cow line and the programme shrinks to the offer alone (M00 section 6).

## 3. The Rule of Three and Four: can Apollo be an effective competitor in farm replacement?

### 3.1 The rule at source

| Source | Verbatim | Label |
|---|---|---|
| "The Rule of Three and Four", Henderson, 1976 | "A stable competitive market never has more than three significant competitors," "the largest of which has no more than four times the market share of the smallest." "Any competitor with less than one quarter the share of the largest competitor cannot be an effective competitor." "The eventual losers will have increasingly large negative cash flows if they try to grow at all." "If the Rule of Three and Four is inexorable, then common sense says: if you cannot be a leader in a product market sector, cash out as soon as practical." "Reinvest in products and markets where you can be a successful leader." "Concentrate." | [BCG-published] |
| "BCG Classics Revisited: The Rule of Three and Four", Reeves, Deimler, Stalk and Scognamiglio, 4 December 2012 | Across "nearly 450 industries" (1976 to 2009) the share of industries with one, two or three generalists "averaged 78 percent"; three-generalist industries earned "an average return on assets a full 2.5 percentage points higher". The rule applies to "'stable, competitive' industries characterized by low turbulence and limited regulator intervention." "Rising turbulence in many industries has also reduced the rule's impact over time." Where it does not apply, companies "should employ adaptive or shaping strategies." Opening test: "Is the industry one in which classical 'rules' of strategy, such as the rule of three and four, apply, or does it demand an alternative?" | [BCG-published] |

### 3.2 Applied to Indian farm replacement

| Test | Finding | Label |
|---|---|---|
| How many significant competitors? | At least five with a visible position: MRF (the name owners search three times as often as Apollo), BKT (a fifth of searches; "over 20%" of Indian agri by its own claim), Goodyear (the fitment leader), CEAT, Apollo, with JK and the cheap tier behind | [Outside-in; Fact - single source; Q3 section 4] |
| Is the largest no more than four times the smallest significant one? | If MRF holds 30 to 35% of replacement and Apollo about 9%, the ratio is 3.3 to 3.9: Apollo sits at the edge of the "one quarter" line | [Computed on planning assumptions] |

But the market fails the stability test, which by the 2012 revisit is the case for "adaptive or shaping strategies" in the parts that are turbulent | [Interpretation] |

## 4. The experience curve and the cost position

### 4.1 What BCG's pages say

| Source | Verbatim | Label |
|---|---|---|
| "The Experience Curve", Henderson, 1968 | "costs decline by some characteristic amount each time accumulated experience is doubled"; "The characteristic decline is consistently 20-30% each time accumulated production is doubled."; "If competitors' market position changes, so do their relative costs."; "Market share must be maintained at all costs" | [BCG-published] |
| "BCG Classics Revisited: The Experience Curve", Reeves, Stalk and Scognamiglio, 28 May 2013 | Does it still hold: "Yes, but in some industries it is no longer sufficient by itself as a blueprint for competitive advantage."; "market share leadership could confer a decisive competitive edge"; "experience can be sourced externally under certain circumstances"; "What has changed recently is that the required speed of cycling between the two has increased dramatically." | [BCG-published] |

| Capacity | India utilisation 92% in H1 FY27 (Apollo's own deck figure, not an industry figure); the only Indian off-highway capacity is Kalamassery, with "limitations in terms of how much it can be expanded"; off-take covers "about 20% to 25% of our agri business" from two partners | [Verified fact: S3 section 2 rows 5, 7] |

What the curve says and does not say here [Interpretation]:

- **Volume is MRF's, and the cost it buys is in conversion, not materials.** Among the big three the FY26 margins rank by size, but FY24 EBITDA per kilogram does not (Apollo above MRF), and in 2026 two-thirds of a standalone rupee is raw material that every maker buys at the same price. The 20 to 30% decline per doubling applies to the third of cost that is conversion and overhead, and MRF has already had its doublings.
- **BKT shows that the edge is cost per kilogram, not price per kilogram.** Its realisation is below Apollo's and its EBITDA per kilogram a third higher. That is an off-highway specialist's experience in a narrow range, sold to export replacement buyers. It is not a position Apollo can buy in Indian farm replacement with marketing money.
- **So Apollo should not plan to win farm replacement on cost, and should not build to.** M02 priced a new farm line at Rs 430 to 790 crore for Rs 60 to 110 crore of contribution a year. The 2013 revisit's "experience can be sourced externally" is the licence for what Apollo already does: off-take for the sizes it does not make, allocation for the ones it does. The programme's supply rule (M08 section 2) stands: no tyre under the guarantee unless Apollo made it and holds its wear data.
- **The curve's one instruction that still bites is speed.** "The required speed of cycling" between accumulating experience and shaping demand "has increased dramatically." In a market where every maker's farm range is the same 12-ply bias tyre at the same load rating (M05 section 2.1), the experience that can still be accumulated faster than rivals is experience of the customer: who he is, how he uses the tyre, when it fails and how fast the claim is paid. That is `C05` and `C06`.

Two things follow for farm [Interpretation]:

- **Farm cannot move any leg.** Six points of farm replacement share (the FY30 full potential) is about Rs 200 crore of revenue, 0.7% of FY26 consolidated revenue, and Rs 42 to 60 crore of contribution, 1 to 1.5% of FY26 EBITDA. Over four years that adds about 0.17 points a year to the revenue-growth leg. A chief financial officer shown only the farm arithmetic should refuse the programme; shown it as Apollo's share of the Rs 230 crore jersey fee, he should refuse it twice.

A farm programme that adds Rs 33 crore of revenue a point with no plant, working capital of a few crore in dealer credit, and a cost that is 90% variable is ROCE-accretive from the first point. BCG's prescription for the industry, "pricing discipline, not overproduction", is also the programme's: no price cut, a promise instead (M05 section 1).

## 6. Invest or harvest: the answer

Putting sections 2 to 5 together. [Interpretation; the decision is Apollo's]

| Option | What it would mean | BCG's rule, as published | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Harvest | Run farm replacement for cash on the fixed plant; no programme; let share drift | Henderson 1976: "cash out as soon as practical" if Apollo "cannot be a leader in a product market sector". But the rule assumes a stable market, and the 2012 revisit says turbulent sectors call for adaptive strategies; the 2014 revisit says "Keep pets on a short leash", not "kill question marks" | Wrong for a sector growing at 7% where two sub-sectors are already Apollo's to lead |
| Invest across the market | Buy share from MRF everywhere with trade money, price or a plant | Henderson 1970: question marks "require large added cash investment for market share to be purchased"; 1976: a competitor below the quarter line "cannot be an effective competitor"; the experience curve puts the cost position with MRF | Wrong: Rs 430 to 790 crore of plant for Rs 60 to 110 crore of contribution; a trade war Apollo cannot win on cost |
| **Concentrate, with a cash cap and a gate** | Lead the two sub-sectors (first replacement on Apollo-fitted tractors; hauliers); run the national offer everywhere it costs nothing until a tyre is sold; test the fixed-cost layers in 30 districts; stop what does not pay | Henderson 1976: "Reinvest in products and markets where you can be a successful leader." "Concentrate."; the 2014 revisit: "invest in more question marks" and "Measure and manage portfolio economics of experimentation" | **Recommended.** It is also what the matrix's own economics allow: a question mark funded from the cash cows at a cost of about Rs 13 crore to March 2028, against FY26 free cash flow of Rs 2,000 crore |

**Status of H1 and H2.** H1 ("farm is a question mark Apollo is already harvesting") is supported on management's behaviour and corrected on growth: Indian farm replacement is growing at or above the industry's rate, so it is a question mark and not a pet, and the harvest is by omission, not decision. H2 ("a specialisation business, not a volume one") is supported through the Rule of Three and Four and the cost position; the Day-1 answer's reference to BCG's "advantage matrix" is withdrawn, because no page on a BCG domain carries that framework and it is described only by secondary sources (S1 row 2.9). [Interpretation]
