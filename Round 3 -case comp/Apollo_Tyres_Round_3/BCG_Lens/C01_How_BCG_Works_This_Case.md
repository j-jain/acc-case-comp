### 1.2 The method, as far as BCG's own pages go

BCG publishes frameworks and findings, not a description of how a case team works. What can be said from its pages, read together [Interpretation; the pages are cited above]:

1. **Start from the environment and the portfolio.** The classics (growth share matrix, experience curve, Rule of Three and Four) and the strategy palette all begin by asking what kind of market this is and where the business sits in it, before asking what to do.
2. **Use the published rule, then test whether it still applies.** Every "Classics Revisited" piece asks "does it still hold?" and answers "yes, but": share no longer predicts performance on its own (2014); the experience curve is "no longer sufficient by itself" (2013); the Rule of Three and Four needs a stable market (2012).
3. **Match the approach to the environment, and de-average the business into parts that need different approaches** (the strategy palette, 2015).
4. **Measure value as total shareholder return and its drivers** (the Value Creators series; the February 2026 tyre study).
5. **Score the odds of delivery, and simplify** (DICE; Smart Simplicity).

What is secondary and not BCG's: that BCG cases are "candidate-led", that candidates should "form a hypothesis", and comparisons of the three firms' styles come from coaching sites and forum posts (S1 section 5) and are not attributed to BCG anywhere in these files. [Secondary on BCG]

### 1.4 What BCG has and has not published that bears on this case

BCG has published one dedicated study of the tyre industry (February 2026: global, listed makers, shareholder returns; nothing on farm, OE, dealers or India by name), a European aftermarket series in which tyres are a segment, a decade of Indian consumer work from its Center for Customer Insight (urban-weighted; one rural study of 2016), two India distribution handbooks with CII, a 2024 survey of 1,000 American farmers, agribusiness value-creator reports that cover equipment makers' returns, a 2026 paper on sports rights holders, and its marketing-measurement work. It has published nothing on Indian tractor owners, tractor or farm tyres, tyre dealers, rural cricket viewing, cricket sponsorship, or Apollo, CEAT, JK or BKT by name (MRF appears once, in a 2014 ranking). So the BCG lens borrows BCG's frameworks, its tyre-industry TSR findings and its Indian consumer evidence; it does not borrow a BCG view of farm tyres in India, because none exists. ## 2. The Day-1 answer

Written on 7 November 2026 from the brief, the first-pass research set, and the facts established in the McKinsey-lens and Bain-lens files, before any new research. It is a hypothesis to be tested, not a conclusion. [Strategic hypothesis]

> **Farm is a question mark in Apollo's portfolio, and Apollo has been quietly harvesting it: the Enschede farm line closed in June 2026 and part of the range now comes from off-take. BCG's first question is therefore not "how should the jersey sell tyres" but "invest or harvest, and in which segment". Indian farm replacement is a specialisation business, not a volume one, so Apollo should fund one de-averaged position it can lead (hauliers, and the first replacement on its own fitted tractors, at the independent counter), win it on speed (claims settled in days, stock in the shop within two days), register every owner at purchase so that the jersey has a known farm fan base to work on, measure the whole thing with a geo-experiment calibrated against a brand-lift read, and justify the money as the test of a repeatable rural-replacement playbook, because a point of farm share is too small to move Apollo's total shareholder return on its own.**

Three reasons, as written on day one:

1. **Portfolio first.** Apollo's cash comes from passenger-car and truck replacement; farm is 5 to 6% of Indian revenue with perhaps a quarter to a third of the leader's share in replacement, in a market that grows with the installed base rather than with the economy. In BCG's growth-share language that is a question mark, and the closure of Enschede and the move to off-take say management has already half-decided to harvest it. A question mark earns investment only if there is a route to leadership in a defined segment; otherwise it is run for cash. [Computed, from M02 and Q3; Verified fact for Enschede; Interpretation]
2. **Specialise, do not out-volume.** The experience curve says cost follows cumulative volume and relative share, and MRF has both; Apollo cannot win farm replacement on cost, and with industry utilisation above 90% it cannot win it on supply either. BCG's advantage matrix would call this a specialisation business: several ways to win, each with a real advantage inside its segment. So de-average the market by district, segment and channel and pick the cells where Apollo's existing assets (its fitment, its truck dealers, its claims line) give it an advantage rivals cannot copy. [Interpretation; BCG-published for the frameworks, to be cited after S1]
3. **The jersey is a data asset before it is a media asset, and the money is justified by the playbook, not by farm's TSR.** BCG's sports work is about turning followers into known, contactable fans. Apollo has no farm fan base; an owner registered at purchase with a guarantee is one. On the economics, one point of farm replacement share is about Rs 33 crore of revenue on a company of about Rs 26,000 crore, so the programme cannot be sold to a CFO on farm TSR; it is sold as the controlled test of a rural-replacement playbook that truck and two-wheeler replacement can reuse. [Computed; Interpretation]

What the client would decide in the first week: whether farm is a business to invest in or to run for cash; who owns the farm profit and loss; and what data it has on fitment, registrations and claims.

## 3. Six hypotheses the research sets out to break

| # | Hypothesis | What would break it | Tested in |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Farm is a question mark Apollo is already harvesting: low relative share in a slow-growing replacement market, capacity cut, off-take; the invest-or-harvest question has to be answered before any marketing question | Apollo's farm share or margin is better than thought; farm capacity was not cut but moved; management has stated an investment case for farm | `C02`; S3 |
| H2 | Indian farm replacement is a specialisation business, not a volume business: the experience curve gives MRF the cost position, and the way for a follower to earn is to lead defined segments, not to chase total share | The segments are not separable at the counter (one dealer, one price list); or cost position is set by rubber and not by scale, so a follower is not disadvantaged | `C02`, `C03`; S3, S5 |
| H3 | Demand spaces (occasion and need: haulage on the road, wet-field tillage, the first replacement at a known hour count, the burst in the season) explain purchase better than demographic or farm-size segments, and the offer should be designed per space | Owners buy on price and availability regardless of use; or the use occasions cannot be told apart at the point of sale | `C04`; S4 |
| H4 | The jersey's main farm use is a known-fan database: registration at purchase, then targeted contact; impressions cannot be priced for a rural audience, as both earlier lenses found | Registration rates in rural durables are too low to build a base; or the rights do not allow the team's marks on the card and the contact | `C06`; S6 |
| H5 | A point of farm share is too small to move Apollo's TSR, so the programme must be justified as an option on a repeatable playbook; a CFO who is shown only the farm arithmetic will refuse it | Farm margins or the installed base make a point worth far more than Rs 33 crore; or Apollo's TSR is driven by revenue growth so strongly that even farm growth counts | `C07`; S3 |
| H6 | On BCG's DICE scale the programme as designed by the two earlier lenses sits in the "worry" zone: long duration between reviews, a part-time team, unclear commitment from the farm sales line, and extra effort loaded on dealers and engineers | DICE's own thresholds, read at source, put the design in the "win" zone; or the earlier lenses' governance already meets them | `C08`; S7 |

## 5. How this differs from the McKinsey-lens and Bain-lens methods

Filled in after the C files were written; see also `C10_BCG_vs_Bain_vs_McKinsey.md`.

| | McKinsey lens (M01) | Bain lens (B01) | BCG lens (this file) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting point | One governing question and an issue tree | One written answer and a workplan to break it | One written answer, then the portfolio: what kind of business is this, and should Apollo be in it at all? |
| First number sought | The size of the prize (M02) | Where the profit sits; Apollo's share relative to the leader (B02) | Market growth and relative share for the matrix; Apollo's five-year TSR and its legs (C02) |
| View of the business | A share-gain programme with layers and gates | An adjacency of the core with hidden assets | A question mark in a portfolio, to be funded as an experiment with a cash cap, in the sectors where the Rule of Three and Four allows a leader |
| Customer tool | Decision journey; coded comments | Net Promoter proxies; episodes; Elements of Value | Demand spaces by occasion; advocacy measured as behaviour (BAI) |
| Channel tool | The offer and the field engine | Sales plays; trade-spend rules; best demonstrated practice | BCG's emerging-market coverage and incentive rules; time-based competition (the two clocks) |
| Sponsorship tool | McKinsey's five measures (2014) | Bain's Zero-Click playbook (2026) | BCG's known-fans yardstick (2026), transferred; BCG's measurement legs and guardrails |
| Economics | Break-even by layer; three cases | Full potential; money map; zero-based funding | The same cases reconciled; TSR and ROCE framing; option value; funding from the trade pool |
| Delivery | A four-arm test with gates; six initiatives; 100 days | Micro-battles; RAPID; the risk dialogue; NPS cadence | The same, scored on DICE and moved to "set up for success"; Smart Simplicity's integrator; a two-person office; the "20 to 40%" prior stated |
| Test of a recommendation | What management would need to believe | The Monday-morning test | Does the approach match the environment, and do the odds (DICE) say it will land? |
| What this method found that the others did not ask | – | The stocking count; the legal guardrail | Whether farm should be invested in at all; what a point of share does to TSR (nothing) and to ROCE (a lot); that no farm-tyre maker has any clock; that the cover need only go where hauliers are; the DICE score |
